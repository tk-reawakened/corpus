# 4) "THE MESSENGER" -- Oyerth

Browse
Short Story Contest - 2007
Written by MrG
I'm not a big fan of elephants. Or masculinity. For some reason, whenever I think of Hemingway, I think of both. But Ernest Hemingway also wrote one of the best short stories I've ever read. Here it is: "For sale: baby shoes, never used." Does it have a title? I don't know. One of the contest stories had no title, right? Bagel's story? That's okay. But, sure, we should probably title our stories. For example: I love bagels. A Chico coffeeshop sells pumpkin bagels. When I lived nearby, I would sit on the shop's porch, eat bagels, drink coffee. Certain mornings, old people would jog by with their dogs--tiny dogs, grizzled people--and I would throw chunks of pumpkin bagel at the dogs to make myself laugh. If I were to write a story about pumpkin bagels, joggers, and oh, I don't know, the persistence of graceless jest, I would probably call it "I Don't Like Dogs." Hemingway drank coffee. That's my segue. I want to talk about how Hemingway wrote the baby shoes story. Rumor has it that the whole gang—Ernie, Dorothy Parker, Faulkner, F. Scott—were sitting around in a Manhattan bar. On a bet, Hemingway doled out the story. Scribbled it on a napkin. See also: shots of whiskey, cigar haze, gruff sadness, etc. All very cute. When I was seventeen, I read a lot of Bukowski and Keuroac and thought that "being a writer" meant weeklong benders, stealing circus tents, hopping log trucks, twanging Mexican hookers, etc. I thought the actual stories, the word bits, would seep from my experience in some romantic, automatic fashion. Like juice? Please don't think that. I'm not sure how else to put it. Just please don't. Read Raymond Carver, Tobias Wolff, Denis Johnson, Barry Hannah, Lorrie Moore. Learn craft. Romance isn't a fever dream. Go ride a public bus and eavesdrop on everybody. Actually, eavesdrop on everybody all the time, sans shame. Notice how funny things are in language. Laz-E-Boy is one of the most beautiful phrases in the world. But listen to the people on the bus. The other day, I heard a man talking about his stereo system. This man wore a velcro-fitted cap and a Tom Selleck mustache. He was talking to this haggard looking blonde woman. We all try too hard. He said: "Man, you should hear the woofers. I mean, I crank it up on Sunday morning for the race, and my neighbors are like shit: turn that thing down! It's great." She said: "It sounds great." He said: "I mean, it's only me." She said: "Oh." He said: "But it's great." And the blonde woman nodded. And Mr. Mustache tried to smile, wave his hands around. It's a good thing I've been conditioned by society not to cry. Real people are in a constant state of heart assembly. Real people are empirically beautiful. I can prove this through chemistry. Real people aren't elves. That's another segue. I want to talk a little bit about science fiction, fantasy, crime, all those "genres" that aren't "literary." Humans have imaginations. Everything else is probably fascism. There. That sounds nice, doesn't it? Humans have imaginations. Everything else is probably fascism. When you go to college, to a writing workshop, you might have a professor who reads a lot of Raymond Carver, Tobias Wolff, Ann Beattie, and so on. These professors may tell you not to write vampire stories, elf stories, crime stories, dragon stories, spaceship stories, Final Fantasy stories, Zelda stories, Future Shock stories, Back to the Future stories, Futurama stories, or Japanese Feudal stories. They are right, sort of. At some point, we learn to reconcile our imaginations with the slush of day-to-day life. You'll know when that happens. The nice thing about humans is that we have three things: a brain, a heart, and an imagination. Stories should come from all three. If you like science fiction, fantasy and crime, read Elmore Leonard, J.G. Ballard, Kelly Link, Ray Bradbury, William Gibson, Octavia E. Butler. I am not kidding. Go Google these people and check them out of the library. But I am kind of kidding. Writing is fun. Language is fun. We like games, right? Language is an arbitrary system of signifiers, a game. Grammar is baseball: ridiculous and luscious and prone to lovely goofs. Stop talking about grammar. Talk about language. Language about life. Life is saturated with humor. How long did it take me to make a Kurt Vonnegut joke after Kurt Vonnegut died? Not that long. And I respect Kurt Vonnegut so much that it hurts my chest bones. But seriousness feels like completion, like a vain attempt to anesthetize life and conclude that you've "figured it out." Don't do that. Never take yourself too seriously. I know it's hard, but cut cut cut cut CUT all signs of melodrama and cliche. Go true. Don't try to write fiction that will make people cry. Write fiction that will awaken mercy. Notice things, what's in the world and how it works. The best jokes and the saddest pictures come with a peculiar feeling, that jolt of overwhelming recognition, of "yes yes yes." Do that. It's not "easy." I'm not sure why it's "important." But when I read that line by Hemingway, that story, something inside of me gnaws his thumbnail, nods, closes his eyes, nods, feels bad about all the hassle he has foisted upon others, opens his eyes, and decides—somehow—to sustain the notion of grace. It's mystical. It's chemical. I am sorry for the homily. Thank you for letting me read your stories. Here is what I thought about the stories. These aren't well-organized essays, just sketches, what I might say if we were in a workshop together. Along with the notes, I've included with each story a little section called WHAT IF, intended to spur ideas.
**4) "THE MESSENGER" -- Oyerth**
We're moving. People are talking. This is good. Stories are made of people and scenes, not exposition. I like this transition: "The messenger said while touching his scarred face. He then pulled out a silenced pistol and shot Mr. Simms in the head. A gust of wind broke Adam's concentration as he stared at his parents' tombstone." This transition makes me think: "you sly dog! I want to know more!" Maybe not quite that. I don't really think in phrases like "you sly dog." I wish I did. Hey guys: what's with all the Sarah? What about a story about Deseree? Or Rhonda? But I like how the bodyguard in this story is named Danny. All the adverbs after the dialogue tags (i.e. "Mr. Adamnan tells him sternly") make me feel like someone is in my living room, rearranging my furniture and trying to teach my dog how to fetch. I understood "sternly" from what was said. Use dialogue, nouns, and verbs, not extra adverbs and adjectives. Trust your reader. Your reader is always smarter than you think. I loved the plot on this one. Things kept bounding here and there. I got a little feeling after I finished, like: "yep, now that's a ." This is good. WHAT IF: What if Adam really doesn't like his dead parents? What if Danny the bodyguard does more? What if people do things like cover their mouths so they won't smell the gasoline, little things that make the scene more intricate and searing ("life-like")?
*story*
**3) "HYDRA" -- Big Red Dave**
This story uses strong, good verbs. Verbs make for good stories. Adjectives make--I don't know, fruit cake. No one likes fruit cake. We like swordfights inside of volcanoes. In this story, I like the sense of something bigger. I like that the story feels "heavy." Not heavy like a hippie explaining the universe via his dreadlocks, but heavy like: we all affect all of us. "Universal" feels like a cheater word, but I mean "universal." Still: maybe a little too much background. It started to feel didactic, a tone that distracted from the strength of "swerved," "gasping," "urgency fired his mind," "buckled." And yet: the last line, stunning, big, beautiful. WHAT IF: What if all that history came not as musing but through scenes? What if we saw the history through tiny, poignant capsules?
**2) "MESSAGE IN A BOTTLE" -- Blob Dude**
Great characters. I am thinking: okay, Jesus, something is happening, I'm worried. This is good. People are moving. That's good. The following paragraph is so good that it punches me in the teeth: "She was right; and as much as I hate to admit it, she is always right. So we gathered what little cash we had and packed some food and clothes into a rucksack and headed for the train station to buy a one way ticket to East Yorkshire." And I dig the bottle trick. Overall, I felt a little like it was raining the whole time, which made me suspicious. Nothing ever rains the whole time. Maybe in dumb movies. But still: I cared about this story. Little knots filled my knuckles when Matt fought Jonny. And that last line: concise, right, lovely. WHAT IF: What if Matt had fucked something up? What if Sarah didn't really believe in Matt? What if they had been doing something funny and fun before the whole ordeal?
Slambang start: “Lock-down confirmed.” “Dammit.” Yes! And what a slick, conversational tone. I felt like this guy was sitting on my couch with a packet of ice on his eyes, gnashing out the story, occasionally lifting the ice so I could see his eyes. Some great lines like "All that blood... I don't want to talk about that." Maybe it was just my mood, but "THEY'VE WRITTEN THE MESSAGE!" made me jump, giggle, pump my fist a little. Okay: the all-caps, the exclamation point, yeah: over the top. But I thought of that girl in Fifth Element, which made me want to keep reading. Part II: Um. I could've done without the perspective shift in Part II. I want the mystery intact, right? Part II reminded me too much of a "story." It's like the guy on my couch turned around to adjust the scenery, and then a smoke machine started, all making me remember "wait, this isn't my house; this is just a set." Keep me fooled, keep me in the fictive dream. The Epilogue, however, works. Thanks to the context of the plot, the epilogue shift makes sense. And another killer ending: "And yet almost hungry. Maybe it'll pass when I get there." I should probably say something hierarchical about why I put this story as #1. Hmm. What should I say? Well, this story did all those time-honed "good story" things: it put me there. It made me feel like I was gone for hours. It made me feel like it was over in a blush. I wish Part II weren't there, but hey: I wish I were a professional tennis player. Nothing's perfect. Speaking of perfect, I saw the discussion about the "Problem Solved" bodies. Don't stress. Take out the word "Somehow." Let me imagine them fixed. Don't make me doubt the reality. Make me forget that it's coming from thoughts. This is a good rule of thumb, anyway. Separate the two. Don't say "When I strolled into my bedroom, I couldn't believe my eyes when I saw two hamsters fighting on my bed." Say: "I walked into my bedroom. Two hamsters were fighting on my pillow. Holy shit." *** All the stories hijacked my eyes and entertained me. Kudos to everyone. Please keep writing. Please don't take yourself too seriously. Please take your stories very seriously. UltimaWizard said it well: "A good writer can write in space." -G** 1) "SOLUTION 9" -- Talyn
