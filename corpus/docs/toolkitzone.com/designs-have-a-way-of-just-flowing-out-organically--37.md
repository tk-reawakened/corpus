# Designs have a way of just flowing out organically when you're holding a pencil

Sick of seeing the same RPGMaker and Final Fantasy VI ripped enemies in Toolkit games? Looking to set your game apart from the crowd? Well, a really cool-looking bestiary of custom monsters can do the trick. All it takes is some imagination and the willingness to sit down and spend a few hours pixeling to get things right. For this tutorial, I'm going to be drawing that mainstay of RPG enemies, the wolf. You can probably do everything in this tutorial with just a pencil and paper and MSPaint, but I'd suggest looking into a program that's a bit more tailored to pixel art. I always suggest GraphicsGale, but there are plenty of other fine programs out there. A scanner can also help things out, but it's not necessary. I'm assuming you pretty much know how to operate your chosen paint program for this tutorial. If you don't know how to operate a pencil tool, fill tool, and zoom tool, um, learn how to do those somewhere else first. Opening up Paint and just freehanding out of your head isn't a great idea. You'll end up with a really deformed character with jagged pixely outlines and flat, boring colors. Here's what happened when I just opened Paint and attempted to draw a wolf off the top of my head. I laid down a black outline without zooming in and filled with the row of colors available at the bottom: I didn't attempt to fix the outline or mix any colors not in Paint's default palette. The result's pretty crappy, but maybe it'd turn out better if I took it into Photoshop or something and used the burn and dodge tools to shade it: Admittedly, that looks a little better, but we can do a whole lot better than that. Now, if you're able to draw at all, pick up a pencil and a piece of paper and do your actual design work away from the computer. Designs have a way of just flowing out organically when you're holding a pencil. If you're completely unable to draw, I'd really suggest recruiting someone to do concept drawings for you. Anyway, the design process is very much a brainstorming session. Do a bunch of small, quick sketches. Every time you start a new sketch, make some changes to the way you did the previous one. Eventually you'll figure out which details work and which ones don't, and you'll end up with a streamlined design that you'll like the look of. Here, I started off just attempting to draw a normal wolf out of my head (without much success) I started a second sketch the same way, changing the pose a bit, but when I got the head, I kind of distorted it out and turned it into a rat head. I also gave the wolf ox hooves for some reason. (heh) From that sketch, I liked the idea of the fluffier tail and the elongated head, so I kept them, but I started to cartoonify the beast a little more to fit in with the style of my game. I didn't like the rat whiskers from the previous sketch, so I removed them, but I kept the rodent teeth and ox hooves. And here's my final sketch. I kept the pose pretty much the same, but took out the stuff that didn't work from the previous sketch. That one had gotten a little too rodent-like for my tastes, so I took out the buck teeth and made the mane more streamlined instead of scraggly. The thing's looking a bit more like a lion now, so I changed the hoofed feet into padded cat feet. So once you get a design you're satisfied with, you can start pixeling it. The design may change even more in the pixeling process as you run into space/color limitations. You can either scan it in and pixel over the top of it, or you could just set the drawing up to your monitor and use it for reference. Again, if you can possibly draw your own design concepts or get someone to do it for you, then do that. But if you can't draw at all, at least do a Google search and find a reference picture before you start pixeling. First, you need to choose which size canvas you're going to work with. You want to the monster to pretty much fill in the whole work area. If you leave too much room around the sides, it's going to get deformed like in my earlier Paint example. You have to judge how big the monster sprites in your game are going to be from a number of factors. What sort of battle system are you using, how big the player sprites are, etc. For this, I'm assuming you're using the default battle system, with its Final Fantasy side view. I'll follow Final Fantasy tradition of exaggerating the monster size in relation to the players. My players are 32 x 64, so a wolf should really just come up about waist high for them and be 32 pixels tall, but I'm going to blow up the proportions and give myself a full 96 x 96 square to work with. (I prefer to use multiples of 32 because that's how big of tiles the Toolkit uses, but it's not really necessary) It's preferable to start with a colored canvas instead of a white one. Pure white in pixel art is pretty harsh to look at and can affect your perception of how colors look when you start filling the sprite in. I chose a nice desaturated green since this enemy will probably be appearing in-game against a forested or grassy backdrop OK, grab your enemy design. There's a couple of ways you can go about using it. If you have a scanner, you can scan it in and resize it to be the same size as your canvas: With the picture actually in the computer and the correct size, you could actually put a layer over the top of it and manually draw an outline over the top of it. (You can really only do this if you're using a program with layers) Be sure to zoom in a lot when pixeling the outline. You have no control over the mouse when working at actual size, which is why my first MSPaint example had such a terrible outline. What this will do is pretty much duplicate your original drawing in pixel form. Here I drew over the top of the scan with a purple pencil, then got rid of the original image and changed the colors around in order to see the new outline more clearly: That gives you a perfectly workable outline, but there's a second method that I prefer. Either open up your scanned drawing in another window and set it next to your canvas, or just set it beside the computer screen. Select the pencil tool, zoom in nice and big, and just go to town scribbling in an attempt to reproduce your drawing. Don't worry about trying to get everything neat looking. Just lay down big globs of pixels that look vaguely like your concept drawing. Erase stuff that doesn't look right, and keep tweaking it until you get a reasonable approximation: What's preferable about this is that you can make adjustments as you're scribbling to make your monster look better at the size it is instead of just being a perfect reproduction of the original drawing, which may not look quite right when it's resized. The problem, of course, is that you're probably not going to want your outline to look that sketchy in the final drawing, so you're going to have to clean it up. Just grab your background color and start hacking away at the outline until you get a clean, 1 pixel thick outline at all points. This is a bit of work, but it produces a nice result: OK, however you went about things, you should have a nice outline at this point. Time to get to coloring. I'll reiterate, MSPaint default colors are bad. They're too saturated and are just harsh on the eyes (especially yellows, greens, and reds). Here's an actual finished enemy from my game with the palette I chose against how it would look if I just filled with default Paint Colors: Just a little too harsh in comparison, don't you think? Mix your own colors. Turn the saturation down (pull colors from the grayish area at the bottom of the color picker) and try to avoid extremes. Pure white and black should only be used in special cases. Try a very light yellow for white and a very dark blue or green for black. Greys should generally have the tiniest amount of color in them (usually a blue or purple) to keep them from looking too flat. For our wolf, I've already used a dark brown for the outline against a light grayish green background. I'm going to fill his fur with a purplish grey, (It's about 8% saturated) his mouth with a desaturated pink, his eyes with a slightly orangish yellow, and his mane with a tealish grey. The white in the teeth is a very light yellowish green, by the way. I've marked each color with a little square on the right hand side in order to keep track of them. Once you've got everything filled in, it's a good idea to go back and change all the outlines so that they're a darker shade of the color they're containing. This just looks better when the enemy is against a background in game, plus it helps to differentiate different parts of the sprite. It's a bit of work to go back and color the outlines, but less so than making them to begin with was, since you can use the fill tool. All right, outlines colored, and the darker shades have been added to the palette on the right. That should do it for coloring. It's now time to move on to shading. All right, first off, I'm going to tell you how not to shade. This is called pillow shading. You start by laying down a line of slightly lighter pixels directly parallel to the outline, then you fill in everything in the middle. Then you pick a slightly lighter shade of the same color and do it again. You continue brightening at regular intervals until you get to the very lightest shade in the middle. What you end up getting is something that looks like it's been stuffed and sewn on to the cover of a baby's book or something. There's no real sense of 3-D, it just looks like it's been mechanically embossed, which it pretty much has been with as much thought that goes into this method of shading. There's a much better method for shading, but it's going to require you to actually visualize how a character's going to look in 3-D and how the different contours of its body would react to light. I suppose if you're having a horrible time doing this, you could find a similarly shaped object and actually look at where the highlights and shadows fall. Anyway, the first thing you need to do is choose a consistent light source. I'm going to imagine my light coming in from the top right portion of the picture. First thing to do is lay down highlights of the portions that would receive the most light. Make a lighter shade of your fill color and lay down patches of color: Due to the texture of the wolf's fur, I've made the lines a bit jagged. I've really only had to add two new colors here, a light grey and a light pink. I was able to reuse the white from the teeth in the mane and eyes. When you're considering adding a new color to the palette, check the ones you already have and see if one of them will do the trick first. Anyway, with the highlights done, the next step is to lay down the shadows. (You could actually do these in reverse order. Whatever you prefer) Make a darker shade of your fill color and start laying down shadows opposite the highlights: This is actually getting pretty close to done now. Some smoothing is really all it needs. At this point, we want to get rid of the remaining jaggy outlines. To do this, we're going to use a technique called anti-aliasing. If you've ever used a brush tool in something like Photoshop, you know that it smoothes the edges around curves by blending into the background with a bunch of transitory colors. Since these sprites need to have transparent backgrounds in game, and the Toolkit doesn't support alpha blending, we can't smooth the outer edges. What we can do is manually smooth up the inner edges by adding transitory pixels between the outline and the fill. It may be a bit hard to see what I'm doing on our somewhat dark grey wolf, so I'm going to do a quick palette swap here to make things visible: OK, look at this jagged line separating the hind leg from the body: To AA, you're going to have to add a couple of colors to your palette. Just lay dark pixels next to the outline on curves, then lay lighter pixels next to those to blend out into your original fill color: I also anti-aliased around the highlight by adding some pixels that were halfway between the fill and highlight in color. The result is something that sort of looks like it was drawn with brush tools, but has the precision that only pixel-by-pixel editing can give you: Now, all that anti-aliasing on our palette swapped wolf ended up adding a few more colors than I actually wanted to, so now I'm going to show you a shading method that can save you some colors called dithering. Here's our old grey wolf again: Instead of picking new colors, you can use the colors you already have and just lay them down in a checkerboard pattern in order to give the illusion of extra colors. At normal size, the eye blends the checkerboarded pixels together to look like a transitory color. In order to get more contrast, I used my lightest white shade dithered in the middle of the highlights and my dark brown shade from the nose to darken the outline in some especially dark portions of the sprite. The result is a fully-shaded, smooth-looking sprite that uses exactly sixteen colors: I guess it's not actually necessary to conserve your color count this much since the Toolkit supports full color. It's just a personal fetish of mine to make my graphics look like those from the SNES era. On the other hand, the less colors you have, the smaller your images are going to be, filesize-wise. If you make sure all your graphics are as small as possible, that can take off quite a bit of size from the final download, which will allow more people to play your game. So anti-alias, dither, or both. Whatever suits your taste. All right, we're done pixeling a wolf sprite. You should be able to take the principles from this tutorial and apply them to other sprites, such as characters, or even to terrain tiles.
