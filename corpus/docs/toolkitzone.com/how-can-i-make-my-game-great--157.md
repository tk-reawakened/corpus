# How Can I Make My Game Great?

**Created May 15, 2006**
We all love to play those big, commercial games that are exclusive to all the big, expensive consoles, but how many of us analyze those games? Many of us here are developing our own RPGs, and even more of us have no idea what were doing. Whats a dream with no reality? Whats a person with no spine?
Half-formed.
Lets go through what makes a game fun, how to implement these things into your own games, and how those big companies manage to produce the high-quality adventures we love.
**I: The Most Important Element**
Before we get into the minute details, we have to ask ourselves a very basic question: what is the most important element in a game? The answer: there is none. Thats right, you dont have to focus solely on graphics or gameplay for every single project you make to produce something beautiful. For reference, lets tear apart a few games; Ocarina of Time, Final Fantasy X, and Sword of Mana, in particular.
Well, if youre not familiar with Ocarina of Time, you can hardly call yourself a gamer. For those who havent had the chance to play it, its Shigeru Miyamotos biggest project ever, regarded by many to be the greatest game ever made. What elements does it focus on? I wouldnt say the story department. The plot is your basic bad-guy kidnaps princess and tries to take over world plot, but it redeems itself a bit by making the characters running the story exaggerated, colorful, and, well, just plain cool. The musics very catchy, but low quality, and the graphics, while good for their time, have always been considered lacking as far as textures are concerned. What makes this giant of a game so incredible? Gameplay, of course! All areas are easily accessed via Hyrule Field, meaning that no matter where you travel, you can always come back to that secret place you couldnt reach before in minutes.
![](library/images/docs/922.jpg)
Not only does it look good, but it also helps to promote exploration  a match made in heaven!
And there lots of secrets, scattered absolutely everywhere  being able to warp through time doesnt hurt the experience, either. It makes hunting down treasure all the more interesting. Even though its the revolution of Zelda, we get the typical eight-dungeon quest  just beefed up a bit. Yes, this is a game known for its endless missions, each one finely crafted. *are*
Ocarina of Times key element: gameplay.
If Miyamoto says that games are interactive movies, then ar-pee-gees are interactive books. Final Fantasy X doesnt just give you incredible visuals and music, it gives you an unparalleled story, the likes of which you can only compare with Final Fantasy VII.
![](library/images/docs/923.jpg)
Come on, you cant tell me that doesnt look cool
While its true that the music is top-notch, and that the games fun to play for its cut-scenes alone, its the dialogue that makes it so wonderful. Unlike Zelda, however, this isnt something youre likely to play for its dungeons. In between Yuna and Tiduss little conversations, youre treated to very bare environments that are about as exciting as watching your friend run on his treadmill for a few hours. Certainly, this game gets its popularity mostly from its emotional drive.
Final Fantasy Xs key elements: visuals, story, and music.
Ah, the little-known Game Boy Advance title, Sword of Mana. Though its been remade into a Mana game, its really just a remake of an old Final Fantasy game for Game Boy Pocket. Its not much of an upgrade; the over-emphasized/over-complicated story and scarce save points make the experience less than fun, but its worth buying simply to ogle at the beautiful two-dimensional graphics.
![](library/images/docs/924.PNG)
Pretty colors.
No, the animations arent all that desirable, but this is still the most stunning game ever to grace the gee-bee-ay.
Sword of Manas key elements: bad story, visuals.
All these games have their ups and downs, and all of them, excluding Sword of Mana, are excellent. Given that youre good in a specific field, youll do fine. When you make a game, let your talents pour into it; dont just emulate. Good author? Drive your game forward with a good plot. Good artist? Amaze players with your homemade visuals. There are no rights and wrongs when youre developing. There are only bad decisions to be made.
**
II: Dont Forget About Fun
Regardless of whether youre good at something, its not going to help any if you base your story around a kids quest to buy a loaf of bread for his ma. Youre going to have to make everything about your game based around the one thing: fun. We play games, primarily, for enjoyment. If you fail to deliver, you fail to make a good game. Designing an area that consists of a long stretch of grass with a treasure chest planted at the center isnt , its stupid. Try making a grassy area with cliffs that can get in the players way, a river that needs crossing, etc. Like in Golden Sun: The Lost Age. When youre running around Airs Rock, youre going to notice the many goodies blocked by various obstacles, and youre going to be compelled to make your way over to them. When theyre planted right in front of you, it becomes less of a mission, more of a convenient station. Whenever you can, make little challenges. As demonstrated in Final Fantasy X, walking from point A to point B cant be called enjoyable. *fun*
![](library/images/docs/925.png)
Dont you just want to scramble over there and open that chest as fast as you can?
Dialogue-wise, of course, this means that long monologues are a no-no as well; you can get away with it in books, but, unless youre really working that script, players are gonna lose interest quickly. Weave a joke into the text. Have some event take place in the middle of a conversation. Every moment of the game, the player should be thinking, Wow, this is so much fun!. Obviously, a game will always have its moments, so some parts will be less incredible than others, but well cover that later.
**
III: When Its Acceptable
Yes, you should know by now to stretch the gameplay to its limits, making otherwise trivial quests exciting and fresh, but you have to know when to do it, when to do it in moderation, and when to not do it at all.
For example, remember the chest across the river? Well, what if the chest only has a really bad healing item in it? The player will be so frustrated that he or she spent so much time getting it that all their excitement will melt away in no time flat. Depending on what youve got in that secret place, you should adjust the level of difficulty. Also, if your party members are discussing a minor event in your game, restrain from making them sound like the worlds about to end. Dramas good, but lets not start making soap operas.
A good example of games that moderate their content is Tales of Symphonia. The dialogue is rather cheesy at times, but characters get appropriately angry when big, nasty cut scenes take place, but calm down not long after.
![](library/images/docs/926.jpg)
Typically, assassins out to kill you arent normally this willing to apologize, but hey, its all good.
This makes the experience feel more natural, especially with the trigger-able skits where the guys get together and discuss recent events. Gimmicks like that to promote your plots not a bad idea, you know.
**
IV: Making it Flow
You cant have cut scenes ten seconds apart, but you cant make them ten hours apart, either. Be consistent. This doesnt mean you have to force yourself to add dialogue all the time, but its a good idea to pace yourself. Players will come to expect a new plot element to introduce itself periodically, to prevent monotony, but when these occur too often, confusion ensues. It may or may not be a good idea to make your game formulaic; if you order your adventure so that the player first enters a dungeon, triggers a cut scene, and then gets to explore a town, he or she will come to expect whats coming next, and then, when the need arises, you break the loop, making big events seemwellbigger. Of course, this is just an example. Many, if not most, developers will choose not to make their game follow such a strict schedule, as games vary widely in style.
**
V: Fine-Tuning Your Game
Now that youve got some helpful hints to give your game a professional flair, lets get into the minute details. This sections will be broken up into sub-sections for better reading, but dont skip things youd otherwise consider unworthy of youre attention.
Characters
Weve seen plenty of paper-thin characters over the ages, and theyre some of the worst things youll find in games. Okay, heres that overused plot we all know and (dont) love: the half-insane guy that wants revenge because some bad dude killed his family. Eighty percent of the time, we see the story executed badly. Besides the fact that it totally sucks, you can still do this right. The problem is, the most depth we ever seem to get out of characters like this is I hate that bad dude, life has no meaning, and Im gonna kill him!. Yeah, you could do that. You could also try making him seem a bit human. Of course hed be angry, thats to be expected, but why not make him feel guilty? Find away to get him alone with someone and talk about his feelings. Make him uncertain. You could also weave a seen where he kills someone to achieve his ends, and is thoroughly shaken by it, but convinces himself that it wasnt his fault and continues to pursue his goal. You cant just take one emotion and expound it over and over again, and you cant show only one side of your character. Anyone can make up some guy with an interesting history, but making him feel like a living, breathing human being is a completely different story.
Religion
Face it: wherever theres intelligent life, religion will pop up somewhere. Youre making your own, private world (unless youre using Earth), so embrace controversy. Its also a great tool for building up your lands and peeps  you can create all sorts of awesome side plots. Maybe the villains really religious, but the protagonists are complete atheists. You dont have to stress it, though; it could simply be that you find a little church in a remote area, etc. If you leave out religion, youre taking a chunk out of the game.
Graphics
Yeah, you knew this was coming. Graphics are the most difficult part of game designing, apart, perhaps, from music. The problem is that its the thing that everyone goes for.
![](library/images/docs/927.jpg)
Your game doesnt have to look this cool to be good.
It doesnt matter. If you can get the job done, thats good enough. Many of the best games out there dont have the shiny visuals youd want them to have. Graphics are really the icing on the cake, but if you just pour some extra sugar into the batter, you can easily substitute. However, I dont recommend ripping. Its like steroids. It may look great, but you cant finish your game, look at it, and say, I made those visuals all by myself. Besides, this limits you to what other games can provide, restraining your sense of creativity. Unless youre content making the typical old-school ar-pee-gee and could care less about originality, draw your own, regardless of how bad it turns out. Besides, its good practice.
Dont Copy
Ripping off Final Fantasy wont get you anywhere. Unless youre some sort of genius, youre not going to get even close to being as good as the real thing, which also means that no ones going to play your game when they can easily pop Final Fantasy X into their pee-ess-two. Your game should reflect you, not someone else.
**
VI: Conclusion
Well, a handful of quick tips never hurt anyone, right? Just remember that some people have a gift for this sort of thing, and some people dont. That doesnt mean you cant be good with a little bit of practice, but its true all the same. Remember to download plenty of other Toolkit games to see what the competitions up to, and remember, above all else, that youre not going to be a crowd-pleaser if you cant please yourself. If you lose interest, thats it.
