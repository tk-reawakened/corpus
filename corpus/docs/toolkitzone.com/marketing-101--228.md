# Marketing 101

This article was inspired by a comment on my last article about Game Development, where the user asked for an article to include marketing. I've gone one better than that, and written a whole article on the subject. Once again, I'll look at this from the perspective of "How the big boys do it". After all, they must be doing something right if they're the big boys, and there is usually a small scale alternative to what they do. Basics first. The whole point in marketing is to get your product lots of attention, preferably all of it good. The big companies do this through several ways, most of which will be explained in this article, as well as the small scale alternative. So without further dancing around the rose-bush, let's crack on. You've created your masterpiece and your good to go for release. But where do you start? Well, getting it online is a good first step. In the Toolkit community we're blessed to have two very good game hosting website's, who'll host your creation totally free of charge. These resources are there, so use them! The two in question are of course right here at TKZ, and also Mystic Software. Do not fall into the trap of submitting to only one of these sites! TKZ might have the flashiest game section, but Mystic Software is famous for its vast archive of Toolkit games. Host it on both of them, or at least make sure there's a link on both of them! Another common trap is to host it yourself on your own site. This might seem to be a great idea, but it's not. Both TKZ and MS have got fast servers, whereas your site (especially if it's a free one) will have limited download rates. Downloads also sap a hell of a lot of bandwidth, so let the two big sites worry about it instead of irritating everyone by not being able to get at your game due to the bandwith being expired. That said, if your running your own site which is halfway respectable in terms of webspace and bandwidth, go for it, as this self hosting approach can be impressive. It's true what they say. First impressions really do mean everything. The first port of call to people interested in your game is generally the Game Profile, especially here at TKZ. Make sure your profile is kept up to date with RELEVANT news items and images. A quick point about News while we're on the subject: write in proper English. None of this "Yea da demo ofmy FreKing wiked gayme is DUN OMFG!!!!111" This sort of slurred, crappy speech is a massive turn-off, and it'll have people frantically mashing the Back button on their browsers. Write in clear, concise English so that we can understand what your saying without a millitary decoder. Also, be wary of bombarding us with news. I don't want to know about every pixel you draw. I do want to know things like possible release dates, and major waypoints in development. It's all about striking a balance. Most people when creating a game also go down the route of creating an accompanying website. This can be a great idea, and the big games companies spend lots of money on complex Flash navigation systems (see the website for what I mean). These systems are often overkill (if you sat there waiting for that site to load you'll know what I mean.) You do not need to master Flash to create a good game website, in fact, tacky Flash intro's and navigation systems can just irritate the hell out of us. Instead, the focus should be on good web design values, with up-to-date news, good images and a way of us lot giving you feedback, be it a Guestbook or even forums. I'd like to say at this point to avoid using things like Freewebs or Geocities for creating your web site. We've all started our web-dev career on things like this, but if you want to be taken seriously, get yourself some proper hosting. Mystic Software is very generous with it's hosting, and many TK game developers call it home. Alternatively, fork out some money here at Toolkitzone for the Uber Membership and get some excellent hosting through here, as well as having the warm and fuzzy feeling of helping out the Toolkit project. Avoid gimmicky fonts and clip-art like the plague. Make good clean images in things like Photoshop or Paint Shop Pro, and if possible make your site in something like Dreamweaver or FrontPage using CSS or Dynamic Templates. An excellent example of how a game website should be done is the site. The impression we get from your website is absolutely crucial, as it shows how much effort your willing to put into your project. The bottom line is, if you don't think you can maintain a project and a website, don't bother. I know I haven't set a site up for my game series, as I prefer to focus my energies on the games Profile here. Adverts are the best way of getting attention to your game, but how do you go about setting these adverts up? The best place to start is by creating a simple, small graphic to put in your signature. Anyone who knows me will know I have a slight crush on Keira Knightley, so I created this for my signature, advertising my website: Something like that in your signature can be really eye catching, and if you take the time to make it look professional, it can really get us interested. I'd advise that you don't make it too big though, or we're fed up with it because it takes so long to download. Another thing; if your creating your image with a lot of computer based drawing in there, avoid JPEG file types, as you will get a lot of artifact on the image. Instead go for the file type I used for the above image: .png which is lossless and yet still fairly small. Another point about signature adverts is to make sure it links back to your game profile! Us browsers are lazy and we will not go to the effort of searching for your game! Another good form of advertising is reviews. Try and get someone to write a review of your game, especially people who write for websites such as tk_gaming. They might not be able to respond right away, but be patient. A good review will work wonders for your games popularity. There are some things when it comes to marketing which should be avoided like the plague. I'll go through some of the worst offenders so that hopefully you won't make this gargantuan mistakes. Do not got balbbing around every forum you can find about your game! This is guarenteed to piss everyone off and will give your game a bad reputation from the start. Small, subtle and interesting ad's and mentions will do the best. Before anyone says anything, there is a world of difference between being persistant and being a pain in the arse. Don't get downhearted if after your best efforts your game still hasn't got that much exposure. The games industry as a whole has several big name games which take away a lot of the limelight from smaller companies. The same is true here. However, if your product is good enough, people will find it and word of mouth is an incredibly powerful tool. Keep the advertising going, and keep your profile buzzing. That way, you'll slowly but surely rake us suckers in. Not so much of a pitfall, more advice. Look around at as many profiles as you can and get a sense of when everyone's going to be releasing their games. If this is your first project, chances are you won't get noticed if on the same day Heraldry, TKLand and Mortalis all make a release. Try and time your releases when there is a lull in the development. These lulls generally come just after Expo's, as the furious creation leading up to these events has now given way to exhaustion. I'll talk more about the Expo's below. If you hit these lull's well, then your game will take centre stage, whereas it might normally be put at the back in the cheese costume in a time of high development. As I said before, the Expo's are a hive of activity, so leech on it! Get involved with the Expo by securing a showcase when they come available, and make the showcase stand out! Pour your heart and sould into that little board, as it might be the difference between sinking or swimming. That said, in your first Expo, you'll probably come under quite a bit of fire, so it's easy to become dispondant. Don't let it get to you! Your first and even second Expo is a learning experience, as you find what works and what doesn't. Each Expo you enter, you'll come out stronger and more knowledgable than when you went in. It's also a good idea to sneak a peak at what other Developers are up to. Know your enemy. Keep trying. Keep pushing and keep improving. If you do that, your project will speak for itself. Happy gaming.
