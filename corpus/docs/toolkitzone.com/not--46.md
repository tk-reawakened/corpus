These elements range from the overall concept of story telling to the most specific details necessary to create a written tale. These articles will each focus on one individual consideration that the writer must master to successfully share a message, ideas or feelings with the audience. This means that it is my intent to help provide you with the wisdom I have gained studying the writing process. It is my intention to make any one feel unintelligent or untalented. My opinions and perspectives are not intended to replace your own nor do I expect anyone to change their minds after reading my words. I love writing and my intent is to help you love it too. I hope you will find this information useful and I hope you will share what you have learned with others who are looking for some insight as a writer. Thank you.**

What is Suspension of Disbelief?
Suspension of Disbelief refers to an audience temporarily setting aside conscious recognition of the presence of fiction in an experience. To be clearer, this means that the reader is drawn into a story to the point that the events within the story seem believable if not real. This is a term used often in the movie business but it also applies to the written word. If an audience can set aside their disbelief of the story, then they will be deeply engaged in your work and you will have succeeded in entertaining them. This is vital if you wish your reader to care one way or the other what is happening in your tale. You the reader to lose track of how long they have been reading. You the reader to not want to set your story down. You want the audience to think about it even after they have stopped reading and you want them to come back. This is how you "hook" a reader. Though that term sounds nefarious, it just means that you have succeeded in entertaining your reader. is your job. is what you are supposed to do. **want** **want** **That** **That**
Make it Real
If you want the reader to lose himself in your tale, then you will have to him. In order to do this, you will have to be interesting. That is not something that can be explained or taught however there are some basics that you can look to for help. First off, you are going to have to get into it . If you want to convince anyone that your story is real and is worth his or her time, then will have to be into it. Practice visualizing events that occur in your story. Imagine every detail that you can. Try to see how the places and people look. Think about how things would smell or what people are thinking about beyond the obvious. Try to see yourself as one of the characters or all of them. How would you behave in those situations? How would you feel? Think about the locations in your story. How do they look? How much space is there? If there are plants like grass or trees in your story think about what they might look like. What time of the year is it? How would that affect how things look? How would that change the temperature? Try and visualize the elements you can see. Try to hear the sound of your characters voices. Hear the noises all around. Feel the textures of fabrics and walls. If you can put yourself in these places, you can describe it to your reader. The audience only knows what you tell them, so its up to you to tell them enough. If you can visualize the smallest details, then you will be able to describe the people, places and things in your story with a much greater confidence. Your confidence will shine though in your words. You believe that your story could actually happen no matter how bizarre or outrageous it is. Everything you are asking of your reader you should ask of yourself. If you want a reader to dive into a pond with you, then you had better be willing to do so first. Once you can see and understand your story, you will be excited about telling the reader all about it. Youll get energized just thinking about it. You wont want to stop writing and in turn the reader will be drawn in by your energy and enthusiasm. If you dont want to stop then the reader wont want to stop . **hook** **yourself** **you**

The Easy Way Out
What you want to avoid is simplifying your descriptions and explanations to the point that the reader can not visualize or even understand what you are telling him. You do not always have to expound on every little detail, but if you do not the reader something then he doesnt it. I can not emphasize that enough. This applies to anything that the reader would not already understand. You can count on the audience to be able to read certain meanings into your words or to think ahead to what will happen next, but they will only be able to do so if they care what the story is about. Never dumb things down. This is a huge mistake that writers can make when trying to be more descriptive. Count on the reader. He has a brain. There is no need to explain obvious concepts or ideas. Learn when to use details. Learn when to assume the reader will understand. Read your own story if you stop and start writing on it again. You have to be into it other wise no one else will be. Learn to draw out suspenseful moments and to layer your story so that more than one thing is happening in a scene. Give your characters personalities and feelings instead of ambiguous labels such as "careless" or "evil". Above all, you must rush though writing part of your story. Why would you do that? If you dont care enough to do it right, then just stop writing. Nobody wants to read something that isnt interesting. **tell** **know**

Suspension of Disbelief is something that must be achieved in order to reach your reader on a personal level. If you can master this, you can gain a readers interest and keep him coming back for more. If you can put yourself in his place, then you will have a better understanding of what you are really asking of the person reading your work. Think about your story as though it real. Write about your story as though it real. Respect your work and respect your audience. Do this consistently and you will always have stories to write and people who will read them. **was** **is**
