Embracing the Cliché
One of the most frequent uses of the General Chat forum is for the proposal of game concepts and ideas. These topics are often ruthlessly dismantled within little time by the baying crowds, occasionally leading to tears before bedtime, and to be fair it is sometimes deserved. Now, I could easily spend a long time writing about the sheer lack of substance within these supposed “ideas”, such as the fact that they rarely occupy more than a handful of lines and often include the sentence “like in ”, but that’s too easy.
The fact is our profession is not storytelling and it’s inevitable we’ll be greatly influenced by what we see and play from the commercial world, that’s why most of us are here in the first place. What I want to discuss is the way we go about dismantling these game ideas and in particular one misguided obsession that in my eyes is always doomed to fail; . *the avoidance of clichés*
So, what is a clich é? According to our good friend Wikipedia;
A is a phrase, expression, or idea that has been overused to the point of losing its intended force or novelty...” *cliché*
Surely the avoidance of overused ideas is a fair demand to make? In theory maybe, but getting caught up in theory is often what’s holding this community back. In theory group projects would work because two minds are better than one, but the reality is rarely as bright and cheerful.
The reality is that to be successful with story telling you have to tick certain boxes, and these boxes, by their very nature, are clichés. How many successful stories, whether it be in film, theatre, literature or gaming, is about a man and a woman from conflicting backgrounds falling in love? Romeo and Juliet arguably kicked it all off way back when, yet today as a premise it’s still used in various guises and contexts and it never gets old. You might say the only time it gets derided as being clich éd is when it’s executed badly.
Telling people to avoid clichés is like telling a baseball player to avoid hitting homeruns, you would be removing a proven component towards success for the sake of being different. Sure, being different can be productive but it’s a lot more effort, highly unpredictable and when you stray from the tried and tested the line thins between a great idea and an awful one. Don’t get me wrong, I don’t think people should stop being innovative but I do think we should be more gracious towards those who stick to familiar territory and not insist they be different because, well, they “should”.
The process of making a game is hard enough as it is, we have to do battle with the coding, graphical and audio elements, and let’s not forget trying to sustain our motivation to see projects through, so why make it so much harder by telling ourselves we have to re-invent the wheel when it comes to story? We allow each other the luxury to rip tilesets when we struggle to design unique ones of our own, so why does this leniency not extend to story? We don’t expect everyone to produce graphics like Khin or 7e, so why should we expect everyone to write like Blob Dude or Talyn? Just remember that the most popular Toolkit release to date is riddled with clichés and familiar story telling but the game works because it executes them incredibly well in a solid all-round experience. We may notice the clichés and yet we still enjoy the game, surely that’s all that matters?
I think we have to give up our obsession with the notion of "avoiding" clichés and just embrace them for what they are; proven storytelling mechanisms. People shouldn’t have to worry about using clich és within their game as t he important thing is what they do with them and the role they play in the game experience. What I think we should be doing on the General Chat forum is asking those who post their game ideas to explore and elaborate upon their clichéd stories because that’s when the cliché can take on a new life of its own and we get a game worth playing.

I'm a writer, but in the circle of my own I've said this exact same thing, and I think it definitely holds true in the realm of games too. Who would still spend all those hard hours of work to make a 2D sprite game anymore, if it were not for all those games we'd played in the 2D sprite game days that we admired? And we admired them, even though many of them were a lot alike. The first thing I heard around writing groups in my early days was "... and for the love of humanity, AVOID THE CLICHE!" (In so many words, anyway.) In reality, what I came to realize was this. What people hate is NOT the cliche itself. What people hate is shoddy work and really bad application of the cliche. True, there are, for example, a lot of games out there where you're rescuing some girl or other. But if that's all you've got in way of detail, I hope you enjoy playing your little Frankenstein, because chances are most people over age 6 will not. A long while ago now, I came up with an idea for an RPG that was supposed to be a little on the cliche and ridiculous side. When my programmer >looks dryly at sibling < opted out of doing anything in the way of an RPG, I found myself wondering what would've become of the characters I'd started designing. I have since actually done the idea into a story which I do when my brain is too fried for my serious works. I was pleasantly surprised to discover that the ideas I'd thought were ridiculous could actually work quite well if I gave them the effort enough. Now it's kind of a personal challenge to prove this very point: the cliche is only bad when badly done. In more capable hands, even the spiky headed guy with the sword could probably come out smelling like roses. Actually it's that mad ball of cliches that brings me here. Still dreaming of getting it into RPG format.
