Hall of Fame
![](http://toolkitzone.com/vault/documents/1254.png)
One of the great things about our community is the fact we remember those who came, did their particulary awesome thing, and went in days gone by. We don't forget the good guys. In light of this, we're starting our very own Toolkit Zone Hall of Fame where each year three users are given the honour of being eternally recorded as having provided a positive and lasting contribution to the community. Although by no means a strict rule, the users selected are no longer active in the community. After this month, when I get a bit of programming time to myself, I'll be adding a special Hall of Fame section to the website. The three inductees will be announced over three Saturdays, beginning today with...
** Christopher B. Matthews
A rather obvious choice, eh? We can't start a Hall of Fame without first inducting the guy who started this whole community off with his hobby project, the RPG Toolkit. Often referred to as "CBM", Chris hasn't been an active part of the community for some years. He handed over the reigns of running the community to me (Xavier) at the start of 2004 and around the same time made the Toolkit open source as he also stepped down from an active developing role. He was on hand over the following year or so as he gave the final say on releases and announced them on the website, but also relinquished those duties in 2005. Being someone who has also created his own hobby baby in the form of the Toolkit Zone, I can only imagine how difficult it must have been for Chris to walk away from the Toolkit. It was a brave decision and, ultimately, the right one.
Since there are plenty of newcomers who might not have even heard of the man, here's a quick history lesson that I obviously stole from his . Chris first started dabbling in the realm of RPG development tools as early as 1996 when, inspired by an article in Nintendo Power, he set about programming a tile editor in QBasic. He clearly caught the development bug and followed up with a board editor, thus beginning his journey to the creation a full blown game editor. The ideas and months flew by and in early 1997, at the age of 16 years, the first version of the RPG Toolkit was released complete with a scripting language he named RPG Code. It was DOS-based, naturally. Under the banner of Awesome Software Online the Toolkit had an internet presence in the days when most homes still didn't even have dial-up and as a result the community was slow to grow. [own story](http://toolkitzone.com/index.php?about&mode=toolkit)
In late 1998 and into 1999, during his first year at University, Chris developed and released the second version of the Toolkit which was Windows based and plugged straight into the heart of a new generation of teenagers getting their grubby hands on the internet for the first time and an explosion of interest in things like programming. This is when the community really began to form and 1999 or 2000 is when you'll find most of the most reknowned oldbies setting foot on Awesome Software's shores for the first time. Over the years the fixes and minor releases came and went, and the Toolkit surpassed a half-million downloads. Awesome Software became Awesome Computing, a registered company, and as production costs increased Chris added a "Power House" version which users could purchase complete with CD and manual.
It was 2002 that the third version of the Toolkit was first seen on the horizon, released eventually in 2003. The Toolkit had begun to fall behind the competition and was in need of an overhaul. The end-result was maybe not as revolutionary as many of the community had hoped and since then the community has somewhat stagnated, but Chris ensured the software still had a future as his final major act was to make it open source for the likes of Colin and Delano to move forward. He was newly married and had a young career in software development after graduating, so he simply didn't have the time to devote to the Toolkit anymore. We all understood.
My memories of Chris were a man who was in it for the fun of development moreso than the acclaim of a community, and while he always appreciated the response he got it was almost as if he was a bit uncomfortable with it. He wasn't someone who wanted to a run a community, he just loved doing what he did best - programming. He was never one for fishing for compliments and ego massaging from his community, he never got involved in spats or fights, he was just a genuinely nice guy. Like so many others here, the Toolkit is the only internet community that I frequent and it's arguably the reason I got into web development and eventually took a Computer Science major. So it's strange for me to think where I'd be on the internet if not for stumbling upon the Toolkit in 1999 and becoming involved in the world Chris created. He's made an impact on the world of hobby game development and can be proud of his achievements. It has been a while since I last communicated with him, I conducted a fun a few years ago also, but this month I'll shoot him an e-mail and maybe I can get a few words from him and find out what he has been up to recently. [interview](http://www.toolkitzone.com/library.php?doc=134)
So welcome to the Hall fo Fame, CBM! I hope many of you take this opportunity to send out some love and kudos to the big man in reply to this article.
