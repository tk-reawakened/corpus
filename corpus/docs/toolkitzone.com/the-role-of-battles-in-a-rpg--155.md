# The role of battles in a RPG

Virtually every RPG is full of conflicts, and fighting them out is an indispensable part of the struggle between good and evil that most role playing games revolve around. In some games we spend hours on slaughtering monsters while in other games the emphasis may lie on different aspects such as the relations between the player characters or the storyline in general, but we rarely encounter role playing games in which there are no battles at all. Taking this in mind, it might be interesting to ask the following question: What different roles can the aspect of fighting have in an RPG? Let us shed some more light on this issue.
Role playing games that revolve around fighting are very common nowadays. In these games you spend most of your time walking around for random encounters to gain experience, money or items. Players spend all their free time trying to get their characters to level 99, and are constantly on the lookout for the best armour, weapons and special moves or spells. The typical RPG of this type usually relies on good graphics and sound, while it is not necessary to have an impressive storyline. If the story is exceptionally interesting, players tend to divide their gaming sessions in level-up monster battling sessions and moving on with the story sessions. Lately, role playing games seem to have shifted into the direction of this category.
There is also the RPG in which the emphasis does not lie on battles, but on the storyline. You spend more time talking to people and trying to find out what to do next instead of beating your foes, except of course a few big boss fights that are important to the story. There are random encounters, but they usually only serve to make travelling slightly dangerous. Moving from town to town, you are more concerned with solving puzzles than with finding equipment and magic or other special moves. Instead of looking up what enemies give the most experience points, you would rather read some extra background information on one of the main characters. These games can afford to have unimpressive graphics; the gameplay and storyline make up for it.
The third category is the category of games in which you can decide for yourself if youre going for the battling and exploring or for the story. Take for example one of the later games of the Final Fantasy series. Players tend to either pull all-nighters trying to beat the game as quickly as possible because the story excites them, or they spend hours and hours trying to beat the optional omega weapon boss or finding that one obscure piece of materia. These games are like works of literature that can be read in several different ways, and they may appeal to different types of gamers. Usually they are all-round when it comes to graphics, sound, gameplay and storyline.
If you are planning on starting a new project, you might want to consider what kind of role the battles should have in your game. Should they dominate your game, or do you want the storyline to be more prominent? Perhaps you want to make it fifty-fifty? Is it going to be a hack-and-slash dungeons-and-dragons game, or do you want to dazzle the players with your brilliant plot? Many people take the battle element of their game for granted, while it could be quite useful to contemplate this issue before you start with a new project.
