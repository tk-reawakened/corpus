How to Draw in Isometric
When you think of an RPG you think of a flat world that you walk around in, such as Pokemon. However, this doesn't have to be the case. An RPG can also be presented to the player in a thing called the isometric perspective. If you are new to making RPGs you may not know what the isometric view is (note that the Isometric view is often called the "iso" view for short). In this tutorial you will learn how to successfully draw a picture in iso perspective. What you will need for this tutorial is a pencil, eraser, and 30 degree triangle. You can print a triangle using this
[image](http://toolkitzone.com/vault/documents/1102.png) and using it as a template by tracing it onto a piece of cardboard (or any sturdy material) and cutting it out. This 30 degree triangle will aid you greatly in drawing your iso lines. Obviously, if you hadn't caught on, you will also need some paper to draw on (any type is fine). Now that you know what you will need, you can finally learn how to draw using this perspective.
Your First Isometric Drawings
If you were looking at the front of a building straight on, it appears to be flat and one dimensional. If you were up in the air at a small distance from the structure, you see it has depth. This is the closest example to an iso view. To be more clear on this here is an example of an Iso drawing:
![ZOMG! A CUBE!](http://thesixthzone.com/vault/documents/1103.png)
As you can see, unlike the front view perspective, this picture has depth. Also note that all features of the drawing are at an equal perspective. I know, you may be saying to yourself "Aw man, this stuff!? I'll never be able to do it!" Well, I bring you good news, it's not that hard if you really pay attention in this tutorial. To start off drawing your beautiful picture you will need all of your supplies mentioned above and the ideal location to draw is at a comfortable desk in a well lit room. To start drawing you need to create a surface to base your object on. You can achieve this by drawing a very light horizon line. If you draw anything there is always a invisible horizon line, it's just that in this case making it slightly visible helps you to start drawing than if you just started making objects on your paper with no reference.
![Draw that horizon, foo!](http://thesixthzone.com/vault/documents/1104.png)
As you can see, the bottom point of the parallelogram is situated on the horizon. The horizon is the base with which you create your object on. The place to draw your first horizon is where you will place your first object. If you want to place it in the top right corner, you would draw the horizon straight across the x axis and n high (how high up the base of your object is). An axis is a line that defines the x, y or z directions (up and down and in) An axis can be visible or invisible. A good example of using an axis is when you graph an equation. You have your x and y axis on your paper. Now back to creating your horizon. You will draw a line across the x axis of your paper where you want to start your drawing. This is how you would go about creating the horizon:
![Make sure your horizon is perfectly level!!!](http://thesixthzone.com/vault/documents/1105.png)
As you can see, like I’ve been saying, the horizon is the reference for your drawing. Horizons aren’t required to be visible, but they can help a bit if you really want to get it right. However, at times in other perspectives horizons are needed to draw but this tutorial won’t get into those. Your next step is to finally start making lines. But before you go off making lines and whatnot, you have to know that isometric lines have to be drawn a special way. Do you remember printing out the triangle template? Well now you are going to use it because, as the name implies, a 30 degree triangle tool draws lines at a 30 degree angle. All lines that would be going left and right in a front view need to be drawn at a 30 degree angle. For this tutorial we will draw a cube. We will start off by drawing the front face, which is a square. A square is equal all around, so all lines must be the same measurement. Putting a square in isometric perspective is like taking it and skewing it at a 30 degree angle. Now you end up with a rhombus (a rhombus is a parallelogram with all equal sides. If you are having trouble imagining this, here is a picture:
![Whoa! It's like stretched, dude!](http://thesixthzone.com/vault/documents/1106.png)
Now the square is in the isometric view. All the lines going left to right are 30 degrees off the horizon. Let's have a closer look at our new isometric square. Note that you need not do anything yet; this is just to demonstrate the whole concept.
![30 degrees = cool like woah.](http://thesixthzone.com/vault/documents/1108.png)
Now that you have the basis for making the iso-square, you are going to draw it. You should already have your straight, level horizon somewhere on your paper. Now you will take your triangle tool and place it level with the horizon with the hypotenuse (the longest side that’s opposite the two legs) at a 30 degree angle from the horizon. Draw a nice bold line about 1.5 (3.8cm) inches long on your paper. You can make sure it’s 1.5 inches by lining up your ruler with it and mark off 1.5 inches (3.8cm). Erase the unnecessary length that exceeds the 1.5 mark. You now have the front base of your cube done. Now take your ruler and make a line going up on each side of the line, measuring 1.5 inches as well. So far if you’ve done this right, your cube should look something like this:
![It looks like a U!](http://thesixthzone.com/vault/documents/1107.png)
If you haven’t already done so, you now draw another 30 degree angled line on the top of the square to close it up. Now you should have an Isometric square staring at you. Note that drawing this square the other way is perfectly fine to, but you’ll have to do everything said here the other way.
Now we will give our square some depth. It is important to note that like I said before, objects in the Isometric view do not diminish into the distance. Everything is equal length. This means that the line that defines the width of the cube will still be 1.5 inches. To achieve this width for our cube, you will take your triangle tool and flip it so that the 30 degree slope is facing opposite the direction of your previous line. That means that, if you drew your first line with the right angle facing the left, you will draw this one with it facing right. I know, this seems like really obvious stuff, but some people need this description. Now draw the same type of line on the top. Finally, draw a straight line down the end, parallel to the middle line, closing the two lines you just drew, and your new half completed cube looks like this:
![Tis is teh uber pwnage!](http://thesixthzone.com/vault/documents/1109.png)
As you can see, all lines coming off of the horizon are angled at 30 degrees. I keep saying this because it is the most important thing to remember when drawing Iso. If it isn't 30 degrees, it's not Isometric. To complete your drawing, you now have to close up the top by drawing a 30 degree line out of the top end of the square you just created. Finish up the square by drawing another line to fill up the gap that's left. Now here is the final product:
![Finally! Completeness! Don't you feel great?](http://thesixthzone.com/vault/documents/1110.png)
Cubes and boxes along with other various shapes, are the foundation of all Isometric drawings. Now that you know how to make boxes in Iso, you can go about drawing other objects that are made of numerous boxes. Try practicing your skills by creating an office building made from boxes. Ok then! Thats it for this tutorial. Another tutorial, explaining more geometry in iso perspective, is possible in the future so stay tuned.
