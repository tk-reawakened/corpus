You might be wondering who Marz is (wow, third-person, never done that before) and what you will be learning from this guide. Alright, alright, so you dont give a flying shit. But guess what? Im going to anyway! Thats right, so before you scroll down I actually have you in a hypnotic trance that will force you to read the following paragraphs and like it! So first, Ill talk about me! Hurrah! Im Marz and many may consider me new, while many may consider me old, while others consider me a midbie. I dont care much for social internet classes, and you probably dont care either, but rest assured I dont know what Im talking about in this guide. If you learn anything, and I mean ANYTHING, I can almost guarantee you that what youve just learned is false. ,br> So what will you learn? As stated above, anything you learn is false. Therefore what I will teach you is a bunch of useless facts that may or may not help you in the making of your game. We will cover everything from Game Design Documents to Programming various little trinkets that contain about two lines of code. So if youre ready, Id like to take you on the ride of your life. If youre a girl, Ill take you on the better ride of your life ;). So whenever youre ready, just click next! Since Im assuming youre new to the TK, and to the community, let me be the first to welcome you to our humble little community. ***Introduction  Who am I, and what will you learn?***
***Welcome to the Community***
The previous sections were nice and short so as to get you accustomed to the whole Youll be reading, so get used to it! idea that I plan on incorporating. Im not an artist, so I cant show you what Im talking about with pictures. Game Design is a very delicate part of making your game. It should always be done first, and if its not done right you can consider yourself screwed later on. What do I mean? Well lets say that youve started a game according to a story that popped into your head one day while sitting on the crapper. You quickly wipe your ass, forget to wash your hands, and are on the computer setting up your newly thought out game. You get so far as setting up a title screen that displays the name of your game (which will be changed many times later) and the New Game button which does nothing. You then head to bed, and stay up most of the night thinking about the different paths your story will take. Finally, you return to your beloved computer on the weekend. You stare aimlessly at the RPG Toolkit Editor and think, What now? And THAT, my fellow newblets, is why having a game design document is so important. This document is the outline of your game, from start to finish. From battle system to tile art. From main characters to mindless NPCs. This document is your games Bible. Whenever you need to know what to do, you refer to this bible. It should contain EVERYTHING! So lets begin on our wondrous adventure in creating this game design document. The first step is to open up word. What you see before you is a blank, empty screen. This is very degrading, and could hinder your thought process. So lets quickly set up a title page. Type in the title of your game in a nice, big-ass font size, and below it type in a one-liner that describes your game (i.e. The ultimate half-life clone!). After that, why not insert a little bit of humour? Game Designing doesnt always have to be serious, so insert a little funny comment thatll make you smile every time you open up this document. After that, you may want to put your name. Once youve done all this, center it all and make it look like a title page. No need for graphics, that just slows down your computer (or mine anyway). So now lets set up a table of contents. Now if you think that I just wrote that all up, you are sadly mistaken. Lets give credit where its due. The above contents were taken from a Game Design Document template I found at http://www.gamedev.net. I cant remember which file I downloaded, but Ive used that template ever since I found it (although I edited it to more suite the Toolkit). Many of the page headings you see in the above contents are pretty self explanatory. When you do get to them, and explain them, make sure youre precise, but dont delve into too much detail. Thats what the Appendixs (located at the end) are for. Each appendix contains every last bit of information that is needed. In some cases, youll need to separate the Story Appendix from the game design document. This is because were making an RPG, and RPGs need good, lengthy stories (most of the time anyway). So lets go on and cover the table of contents. Please not that I will not do obvious sections since thats just a waste of my time, and Id rather waste my time playing Warcraft III. Under the Design Foreshadowment section youll notice Version 1.1, 2, and 2.1. These are to get you under way and set goals for each of your versions. The first number signifies a major revision. The number after the decimal point is signifies minor revisions. Thus, when we say Version 1.1, that means its the first major revision, and that its undergone one minor revision. In the game well be creating, it will undergo one alpha, two betas, and then finally release the full version. 1.1 will be our first alpha. 2.00 will be the beta we release to select testers, and 2.10 will be the one we release to the same select testers with bug fixes and a few tweaks here and there. Up until now, Ive bored you with perhaps the most useless information youll ever read. But now we move on to the fun part, coming up with an actual game. So lets go over a small FAQ: Now what is this dream game? Well, to keep things simple, and because this is our first game, well have to hold back on all the amazing features that I can hear you all shouting (e.g. 3D cut scenes like in Metal Gear Solid!). So what is this game were creating? Well, to make an RPG it takes all qualities of most games. Like in Action Games, we need good graphics. However, we cant deliver this. The purpose of the RPG Toolkit is to create games like those Final Fantasy games for the Super Nintendo. Thus, we will be making graphics at under that caliber because Im not an artist and cant create graphics that are similar to Secret of Mana. In fact, you may want to think of the graphics as a sub-par Zelda game, at least thats what I hope the final product will be. RPGs also need a good story, just like in Adventure games. The story doesnt need to be revolutionary, but you should try your best to avoid clichés and make a story that will drive the user to continue the game without a sense of boredom. Remember, this is a game, and you want the user to have fun. Ive decided on making a traditional Fantasy RPG since that is probably the easiest thing we could do, and its the most popular with the RPG Toolkit. The game takes place in the world of Lamber, and you can control one of many characters and character classes. Throughout the game, you will meet several different NPCs, some of whom may react timidly or boldly. The game will use the tested, tried, and true formula that gave the pokemon series so much fame and loyal followers. No, I dont mean the creatures that you get to raise and catch and what not. The formula is you taking your character through different cities, battling people as you go along the way. Once you reach the city, you get to face off against a boss. Once youve visited all the cities, you get to fight the main boss (I think they were called the Pokemon Elite or something like that). We will be incorporating this formula into our game: Many Cities (Sub-Bosses) + Many Combatants + Final Boss = FUN! Of course, it wont be much fun if our characters suck almost entirely, but thats not the point. Or maybe it is? The game features many features as I try to expose you to all that the TK is capable of. However, Id like to include a simple point. In our community, its become some sort of custom to mention only the features that most other games dont include (e.g. Custom Battle System). However, features mean everything the game has to offer. So, lets take a look: We will delve into these features now! Depending on how far we get in this game, I might teach you how to insert some multiplayer into your game. Before you go off thinking Im going to teach you how to make a Massively Multiplayer Online Role-playing Game, think again. I will simply show you how to make a connection from one computer to another, and perhaps get a little bit of a game going. If I do decide to implement a multiplayer feature, the bare minimum Id like to do is a little 1 v 1 battle where you take your save game character and fight against your friends character. Since our battle system is turn-based, this isnt anything revolutionary. Alas, its still an online multiplayer feature. If I decide to go the full mile with this whole online connectivity bull shit, we might make a few mini-games online; create Tag-Team battles, etc. But dont hold your breath, this probably wont happen anyway. Some of you may be ecstatic that were using the RPG Maker tiles. However, most of you are probably disappointed. For those who are disappointed, heres a little bit of reasoning. Although the RPG Maker tiles are technically illegal to use, this isnt a commercial game, and is for the sole purpose of bad education. Secondly, the RPG Maker tiles provide a wide array of professionally made objects, terrain, etc that have been used poorly by many other games. I intend to teach how to use them properly. Thats right; were going to learn how to make GOOD boards in this tutorial. When I was first a newbie, I followed many of Seafroggys tutorials, and agreed with many of his arguments. Since then Ive developed my own likes and dislikes for boards, and what can make the boards perfect. Board making is so overlooked in TK games that most people dont care anymore. What people dont understand is that the boards you make IS your gaming world. You want to breath life into your gaming world, not just make it a place where you randomly walk and see the occasional tree. I plan on incorporating two mini-games in this game, although I might add more later. The mini-games are nothing special, just something the player can do after battling with his many rivals in the game. The 1st mini-game is a simple battleship game. The player will attempt to sink the opponents battleship in a limited number of moves. If the player wins, hell get some sort of cash prize. If he loses, hell lose the money it cost him to play the mini-game in the first place. Note that there is no actual battleship. The mini-game will actually feature many different objects and names because, quite frankly, there were no battle-ships during the time setting of this game :P. The 2nd mini-game is more of a game-world mini-game. What this means is that the mini-game increases in difficulty as the player continues from level to level. Still dont understand? How about an example: Dark Age 2 had a card game. Wherever you went in the demo, there were always a few NPCs willing to take you on in a game of cards. No, we wont be making a card game. Instead, its a chess game. A Fantasy Chess game. Ill delve more into the details of this later, since its not that important, and it may not get done anyway. The battle system for this game will be relatively simple. You will be able to attack, use skills, use items, and surrender. Also, for funs sake, we will be adding a Morale meter. The Morale meter is a power bar that relates to how many battles youve won and lost. If youve just won 4 battles in a row, your morale will be high, thus allowing you to get more critical hits. If youre morale is low, the player will have to seek out weaker fighters so that they can boost up their morale again. If youre wondering how this works (although I still havent taught you how to program), Ill show you. We will record all the battles that the player has won and lost. When the player wins a battle, we record it as a Winning Streak of 1. If he wins his second battle, we add one to his winning streak. Thus its is now a Winning Streak of 2. When the player loses a battle, it will become Winning Streak of 0. And then we record the loss, showing Losing Streak of 1. I think you get the idea. Now, if the player has won his last 3 games, well give him a morale bonus. If he lost his last 3 games, well take away from his morale. Its very simple really. ,br> That is perhaps the closest thing to we will have to an amazing feature in our battle system. Everything else will be straight forward. The custom menu system will feature our quest system built right into it. The Shell (when I say shell, I also mean menu-system. I use the 2 words interchangeably) will feature the basics where you can view your characters stats, what weapons hes wielding, what armor hes wearing, what items hes holding, etc. Menu systems arent hard to make, all it is, is a bunch of stats organized into different areas. Our quest system will show the characters Main Quest and his side quests. His main quest will always show what city he has to go to next. His side quests will show any quests given to him by some random NPC. For example, one particular NPC wants the character to get her necklace that just fell down a well. This would be shown in the side quests column, and would also provide the location of where the character got the quest. If a quest is completed, the character can view what rewards he received from it, etc. There will be many different classes available to the player when the game starts up. There are four attributes: Energy, Agility, Intelligence, and Power. Energy determines how long the player will survive in battle. If his energy is high, it means he can take a good beating before he goes down. Agility is how many attacks the player can get in before his opponent attacks him. If he has a high agility, the character may be able to kick the opponents ass before even getting hurt. Intelligence determines how smart your player is, which relates to how many skills he can use. If your character is very intelligent, then he will be able to access a wide variety of skills that will aid him in battle. Lastly, Power is the sheer strength of your character. It relates to how much damage your character does with a normal attack. If your character has a lot of power, a single blow might destroy your enemy. Now let me give you an example of how leveling up works. If you chose the archer class, the primary attribute is Agility. The secondary attributes are power and intelligence. And the tertiary attribute is energy. This means that the agility stat will increase the fastest as the character grows in level, and that energy will be the slowest. Each class is different, but you may find some that are very similar in attributes. Another way Ive devised to distinguish between characters is what kind of skills they are able to obtain. A warrior may be able to wield two weapons, while any other class cannot. A rogue may be able to obtain the skill for invisibility, while no one else can. There will be skills that overlap between classes, but each class will have their own special skill that they can use exclusively. Also to-be included is a skill points system much like Diablo II. When you level up, you will receive a number of skill points to place into new skills or old skills. Placing these points into old skills will allow you to make them more efficient (for example, making your warrior more efficient in wielding two weapons thus causing them to give more damage). Place these points into new skills will allow you to use a brand new skill. Throughout the history of gaming, many different games have tried different ways of having the player communicate with NPCs. In some games, talking to an NPC meant hearing the same thing over and over again until you did something special (like in Zelda). In other games, you were given a list of topics to ask about, and the NPC would discuss these topics. Some detective/adventure games had you asking a series of questions so that you can get the correct answer. If you didnt get the answer, you had to start asking questions from the beginning again. Due to the complexity of the NPC conversation system, however, Ive decided to delay its explanation until we know a little bit more about the TK and RPG Code.

***Feature Set***
- Custom Battle System  The game will feature a relatively simple custom battle system. Were not trying to reinvent the wheel here, just create a battle system that works and works well.
- Custom Menu System  It would seem relatively weird if we had a custom battle system without a custom menu system. Also relatively simple, it just takes a while to get everything together.
- Custom Level-up System  As is customary, a battle system isnt a battle system if youre characters dont level up. Well go over this as well.
- NPC Conversation System - We will be programming a relatively simple way for communicating with NPCs. Some games have NPCs talk to you according to the situation at hand (situational conversations), while other games have NPCs that talk according to selections (e.g. How do I kill so-and-so?). We will be discussing the advantages and disadvantages of both and then deciding on a nifty little compromised system.
- Quest System  We will be creating a way for the player to keep track of their quests. That way they know which quests are necessary for the story to continue, and which quests are for the side and should be done for fun.
- Mini Games  We will feature about two mini-games in the game. Nothing special, just some games that will give the player something to do to have fun away from the actual game.
- RPG Maker Tiles  Did you really think I was going to learn how to make tiles for this?
- Tournaments  Because when the character finally reaches a city, he will get to fight a mini-boss, we will be implementing a tournament method. One which tests the players endurance by pitting him against a number of fighters before he reaches the Boss in a tournament style.
