# OC Custom Stats

Bravo to OC. Great tutorial here on how to pretty much make custom everything. The best part is I only found 2 mistakes!
#Method Table_Of_Contents() { #introduction!=1 #my_stat_theory!=2 #naming_stats!=3 #assigning_stats!=4 #gotta_love_arrays!=5 #level_up_up_and_array!=6 #the_menu!=7 #battle_tactics!=8 #conclusion!=9 #credits!=10 }
#Branch(:introduction)
Hey, OC here (with my third tutorial. Woo!). Today I'm going to discuss Custom Stats. This is here for those of you who want to break away from the old stats from the toolkit, and get into something totally your own. Making a system of Custom Stats for your characters is one of the best ways I know to spruce up your game, adding originality and complexity, and allowing you to be completely free with custom Menu's, and battle systems (which I'll cover later on in this tutorial). Making a game with good custom stats could easily boost the respect of your game and get people more interested. Because, as I've learned through the time I've been in this community, people like custom made things. So get out that RPGCode editor of yours, fire up the toolkit, and get ready to make some stats!
#Branch(:my_stat_theory)
If you're going to be doing custom stats, you'll want to do everything custom (all stats, battle system, and menu). What I do with my custom stats is I make a file that has all of the player stats, a file that has the level up programs, one that stores the information about the player special moves, and one that has the information about what special moves you recieve for level ups and stuff. I leave all of the character stats to their default and run my custom stats in the battle system and menu, so you won't even know that the original stats are there. Because of this, the original stats set by the toolkit never get changed. So you'd need to do (as I said before) everything custom.
#Branch(:naming_stats)
Ah yes, the simple yet delicate art of naming the stats. When making custom stats, you'll want a system of what order they go in, what they are called, and what each one does. This is for easy reference (in case you're writing them in the middle of the night and forget what they all mean in the morning). This also adds a sense of good organization to your stat system. So, let's plan out what stats we're going to have. Right now, I won't add any very complex ones, just the normal ones from the built in stat system.
HP/MaxHP - the health points (or hit points) of the player. I'm sure everyone knows why this is here, so I won't bother explaining it any further.
SP/MaxSP - the special points. These points are given to your character so he or she is able to use special moves. When a special move is used, SP is deducted from the amount of MaxSP.
Att. - the attack power. Attack power is what makes your characters strong. A high att. power will inflict more damage on the enemy. A low att. power will inflict less.
Def. - the defense power. This causes your character to defend themselves in battle. The higher the def., the less damage will be done to them. The lower the def., the more damage is done.
Level - the level of your player. When the level gets higher, all of the stats increase. So your character gets stronger, more health points, more special points, and defense is up also.
TotalEXP - the total experience points. Without experience points, you wouldn't be able to get higher levels. So this is a crucial stat to include. EXP increases when you beat enemies (usually). But you can do something different if you want to.
NeededEXP - the experience points you need for the next level. Another crucial stat to include; it holds the total of EXP that you need to advance in levels.
Now we have names for all of our stats, and we know what all of them are supposed to do. So let's move on to assigning different stats to characters, shall we?
#Branch(:assigning_stats)
In a stat system, assigning the stats to characters is the most important thing you will do. You will need to decide what attributes the players have. Some people might want one character that's heavy on the Att. Power, and maybe low on Def. Or, maybe you want a character who's really good at defending, but not so big on attacking. Well, it's all up to you! But I'm going to give you some tips on appointing stats, so you won't be completely in the dark about it. Hehe.
There are a lot of different types of players you'll see in games. Here's a small list of some of the types of players and their attributes;
Healer: Low HP and Att. Power. High SP and moderate Def., though, so they can use lots of healing spells. Physical Fighter: High HP and Att. Power. Not very high SP, and moderate Def. Spell Fighter: Moderate HP, Att. Power, and Def. Very high SP. All Around: Moderate HP, Att. Power, Def., and SP.
So now you have some idea of what kinds of characters you can make by assigning them stats. You can also mix those up and make variations on what I have on the list, so it will lead you to having even more options of what people are like on your team.
#Branch(:gotta_love_arrays)
If you aren't familiary with Arrays, I suggest you go and look at my tutorials on how to use them. Because this next part here will be dealing with arrays. If you do know arrays, then keep on reading. (If you can't tell already from seeing that I wrote two tutorials about arrays, I'm obsessed with them. Such useful little things!)
So far you've learned about naming the stats, and assigning them to characters to create a little bit of their personality. Now you're going to learn how to assign them. This is where I want you to pull up notepad, or whatever you're using, and follow along. 'Cause you're going to learn some important stuff about custom stats. And that is... databases! I like to store all of my custom stats in databases. You know, like having character stats in one program, enemy stats (for a certain level) in another, and such things like that.
Let's say that I want to create a character named "Bob" and he's going to be an all around type of guy. So let's make all of his stats!
*Stats for Bob #playerName[0]$="Bob" *Stores Bobs name in slot 0. #playerHP[0]!=50 *Stores the current HP in slot 0. #playerMaxHP[0]!=50 *Stores the max HP in slot 0. #playerSP[0]!=10 *Stores the current SP in slot 0. #playerMaxSP[0]!=10 *Stores the max SP in slot 0. #playerAtt[0]!=10 *Stores Attack Power in slot 0. #playerDef[0]!=10 *Stores Defense Power in slot 0. #playerLevel[0]!=1 *Stores the players starting level in slot 0. #TotalEXP[0]!=0 *Stores the players total EXP in slot 0. #NeededEXP[0]!=50 *Stores the EXP needed to gain a level in slot 0.
Ok, now you've stored all the statistical information for Bob, but where do you put it? Well, save the file as CharacterStats.prg or something along those lines, and include it with the #Include command into your start program. Now the stats for Bob are accessible.
#Branch(:level_up_up_and_array)
Now that you've added the Stats into the game, you can leave that part alone. Next thing you have to do is make a level up program for your characters. You might want to add some graphics for your level up system, so it's not just a bland n' boring you got so and so. But this is just to give you an idea of how to make your own level up system for the characters. Here's an example of a level up program:
#Method Bob_LevelUp() { #playerLevel[0]!=playerLevel[0]!+1 #NeededEXP[0]!=NeededEXP[0]!*1.5 #playerMaxHP[0]!=playerMaxHP[0]!+6 #playerMaxSP[0]!=playerMaxSP[0]!+3 #playerDef[0]!=playerDef[0]!+2 #playerAtt[0]!=playerAtt[0]!+2 }
As you can see, on the level up everything is raised. HP, SP, Att., Def., Level, and NeededEXP. It adds 1 to the level, it multiplies the NeededEXP by 1.5 each time you get a level up, so it steadily increases, and I'm sure you get the gist of what everything else does. If you're new to this kind of thing, you might ask yourself why does playerLevel[0]! equal playerLevel[0]! and then add 1? Well, the answer is simple enough. In programming, what happens on the right side of the equasion gets put into the left side. So, that makes playerLevel[0]! equal itself plus 1. The same goes for everything else on there.
Now on to the next part of the level up: the calculation of special moves. What we're going to do now is make yet another file, and this time we'll put the information for some special moves in there. Let's say that Bob has two special moves throughout his levels. Shall we store them in their own arrays?
#Bob_SpecialMoveName[0]$="Bob Super Attack1" #Bob_SpecialMoveAtt[0]!=Att[0]!+5 #Bob_SpecialMoveAnm[0]!="BobSuperAttack1.anm" #Bob_SpecialMoveSP[0]!=5 #Bob_SpecialMoveEXP[0]!=0
Alright, now you have his first move. The SpecialMoveAtt is the attack power of the move. What it does is it takes your current Att. power and adds 5 to the total, so the attack is stronger than a normal attack. The SpecialMoveAnm is what you use to display the animation for the attack. So it's used like this:
#Animation(Bob_SpecialMoveAnm[0]!,x!,y!)
That's for the battle system, so we won't go into that anymore just yet. The other thing you'll need to take note of is SpecialMoveSP. That's the useage, so it's important. It's set to five, so when you use the move, it takes away five SP points from Bob. The EXP is set to 0. That means that Bob can always use this special attack. Now, let's add another special move to the roster.
#Bob_SpecialMoveName[1]$="Bob Super Attack2" #Bob_SpecialMoveAtt[1]!=Att[0]!+7 #Bob_SpecialMoveAnm[1]!="BobSuperAttack2.anm" #Bob_SpecialMoveSP[1]!=8 #Bob_SpecialMoveEXP[1]!=NeededEXP*1.5
The only thing that's really different about this one is that the EXP equals something different. Now, it's set to NeededEXP multiplied by 1.5, so obviously the needed level for this special move is 3. Now, we'll do the level up program again, but this time we'll make some changes. Watch closely.
#Method Bob_LevelUp() { #playerLevel[0]!=playerLevel[0]!+1 #NeededEXP[0]!=NeededEXP[0]!*1.5 #playerMaxHP[0]!=playerMaxHP[0]!+6 #playerMaxSP[0]!=playerMaxSP[0]!+3 #playerDef[0]!=playerDef[0]!+2 #playerAtt[0]!=playerAtt[0]!+2 #Bob_SpecialMoveLevelUp() } #Method Bob_SpecialMoveLevelUp() { #If(TotalEXP[0]!=>Bob_SpecialMoveEXP[0]!){ #Bob_SpecialMove[0]!=1 } #If(TotalEXP[0]!=>Bob_SpecialMoveEXP[1]!){ #Bob_SpecialMove[1]!=1 } }
I'm sure you can tell what's different about it. I added a new Method, which see's if Bob's Total EXP is equal to or over the special move EXP. If so, SpecialMove[0]! equals 1. And then it looks at SpecialMove[0] in the same way. Now, you might be thinking, "Why are there two more Special Move things?" And the answer is: because you'll be using these in your menu system. Which I'm getting into right now!
#Branch(:the_menu)
Ok, so I'm not going to teach you how to code an entire menu for yourself. But I am going to give you a helpful hint about reading in special moves. What you saw above is just that. In your menu system, where you're going to be displaying Bob's special moves, write this out:
#If(Bob_SpecialMove[0]!=1){ #Text(x!,y!,Bob_SpecialMoveName[0]!) } #If(Bob_SpecialMove[0]!~=1){ } #If(Bob_SpecialMove[1]!=1){ #Text(x!,y!,Bob_SpecialMoveName[1]!) } #If(Bob_SpecialMove[1]!~=1){ }
That will display his special moves (if he has them, of course). You'll need to do this for all of the special moves that your characters have. It gets a little repetitive and annoying, but you'll be proud of yourself when you're done with it. Also, you'll need to include the Stats program, Level Up program, and Special Moves program into the menu, so you can display all the stats n' stuff.
There's one more thing you'll need to know about the Menu system and using your custom stats. This question was brought to me by Dandy Masher (haha, I mentioned you). Here was his question: "Occasionally_Correct, I see that you labelled your characters as 0 through 4. Does this cause a conflict if you put your characters out of order? For example, can you put character 0 in the fifth slot in your character party without causing a conflict?"
And the answer is yes, you can. And this is how you do it: use the normal method of changing characters around (the #removeplayer and #addplayer commands). Make sure that the name of the player you set in the character editor of the toolkit and the name you gave them when handing out stats is the exact same thing. Here's what you'll need to do after adding/subtracting characters:
#If(playerhandle[0]$=playerName[0]$) { *Display the information on the menu for playerName[0]$, *including stats, name, and profile image right here. } #If(playerhandle[0]$~=playerName[0]$) { #If(playerhandle[0]$=playerName[1]$) { *Display the same stuff as you did before, except it needs *to be the information for whoever is stored in slot 1 in *the stats program. } }
What that does is checks to see if playerhandle[0]$ equals the starting player. If it doesn't, then it knows that you switched the player order or just got rid of one of the players, so it checks if playerhandle[0]$ equals another player. If it does, then it loads another players information onto the screen where the starting players information use to be. You can keep repeating the process if you have more than one player switch in there. This is a very useful thing if you go from one party, and then switch to another party with different characters later on in the game.
#Branch(:battle_tactics)
And now, the part you've all been waiting for. It seemed like a trend not too long ago that everyone wanted to start making battle systems of their own. The only problem is, they never knew how much trouble they were getting themselves into (well, the newer people at least). Anyway, this next section is dedicated to lightening the load of making a custom BS. No, I won't code the entire thing for you, but I will show you how to use those custom stats in here (in various ways).
The first thing we're going to cover in the battle system section is an attack, using a special move. Want to see what it looks like? Alright then! (NOTE: You might need to read through this part a few times before you completely understand all of it. But there's a big paragraph of explanation for you, so don't worry)
*Attack using a special move #AttackingEnemy!=1 #While(AttackingEnemy!=1) { #If(playerSP[0]!Bob_SpecialMoveSP[0]!) { #Animation(Bob_SpecialMoveAnm[0]$,x!,y!) #Bob_DamageDone!=Bob_SpecialMoveAtt[0]!*3/EnemyDef[target!]! #EnemyHP[target!]!=EnemyHP[target!]!-Bob_DamageDone! #playerSP[0]!=playerSP[0]!-Bob_SpecialMoveSP[0]! #AttackingEnemy!=2 } }
That's such a simple program, I'm not even going to explain how it works to you. You can do it on your own. Haha, just kidding. Ahem, anyway... As you can see I used a loop to initiate the attack. You'll have to change that though, so it fits in with your own battle system. After it goes into the loop, it asks if you have enough SP to perform the move. If not, then it exits the loop and lets you go back and pick another move. If you do have enough SP, then the first thing it does is plays the animation for the attack (as you can see, I used the same array that we made for the special move animation). After that, a new variable is made which is Bob_DamageDone!. What that does is puts the Att. Power of the attack into it, multiplies it by 3, and then devides it by the enemies defense power. A little confused on what it runs through so much just to get the damage that was done? Well, let me clear it up for you. If you don't do it that way, then Enemy defense is pretty much useless, so you have to use that. And I multiplied it by 3 because if I just had it without that, the outcome would have been 3HP damage done (10 + 5 added by the special move and devided by the enemies defense, which is 5 for this example). Now do you see why I did that? That makes the outcome effect, instead of 3, 9. Cool, huh? The next thing that happens is that total amount (stored in damagedone) is deducted from the enemies HP. So now, the enemy has 9 less health points. Last but not least, the SP used by the special move is deducted from your total SP and it breaks out of the loop.
Since this is becoming a lot longer than I thought it would, I will teach you how to do one more thing. This is a simple status effect that you can use as a guideline to making more complex status effects for your custom battle system. So here we go:
#Method StatusE_drainallsp(target!,ene_num!) { #EnemySP[ene_num!]!=EnemySP[ene_num!]!+playerSP[target!]! #playerSP[target!]!=0 #If(EnemySP[ene_num!]!>EnemyMaxSP[ene_num!]!) { EnemySP[ene_num!]!=EnemyMaxSP[ene_num!]! } }
That's a simple program (and this time I'm not kidding!). I'm sure that it looks confusing to new people, but it's really not. Let me explain what it does. First, it makes a Method called StatusE_drainallsp, which takes two parameters (target! and ene_num!). target! is the target selected by the enemy (the person they're using the move against), and ene_num! is the actually enemy slot. The first line of code adds the targeted players SP to the enemy SP. Then the second line sets the players SP to 0. After that, it checks if the SP of the enemy is OVER the specified max sp. 'Cause we can't have that, can we? If it is, it sets the enemies SP to max, and then it ends the method and goes back to the fighting.
