The Art of Reviewing Toolkit Games (ARToG)
- By Seafroggys Copywrite 2004 Spiff Software
I hate most reviews. People can't review worth crap, and they give nice scores to crappy games. They aren't very critical. I don't know if they base their 'scores' off of what their game is like (assuming their game will be 100%, of course, and assuming that their game gets done to what they have in their mind, not what comes out on the screen), or if its upon some median perscribed by the average Toolkit game. I'm fairly notorious, or used to be, as its been two years since my status as a reviewer has been in existance, before Toolkit Zone went under for the first time. Anyway, I'm fairly notorious as a harsh reviewer, giving the single greatest game (D&C 1.1) a 77%, and most games I've reviewed in the 50% range. Yes, in a way, I compare them to what I see in my games, but also, I compare them to what they could be. I see them as games; most other reviewers see them as interactive fiction. I see them as cliched; they see them as having great stories. Obviously, in most community reviewers' mindsets, they think all these games are great, but I see them from a completly different viewpoint; as games, which is what they're meant to be. I'll elaborate on these philosphies later, but first, the breakdown of my reviewing scale (which is a great model for you to use). Many many reviewers and critics of Toolkit games have dismissed graphics as the primary focus of the game, instead relying on the underlaying story (or in my case, game). That is fine, but it bugs me when community members dismiss graphics entirely or they put it underneath other aspects, like story. I'm a very fair person. As all catagories are rated from 1 to 5, they should be equal. And that should be so. Good graphics should be as important as music and story and gameplay. So what is defined in this catagory? Individual tiles, how well they are drawn, and how they work together (do they clash?). Are the sprites good? How about bitmaps and cutscenes? Are they cheezy (and don't use that word, I was given hell about it in one of my first reviews)? Surprisingly, this is one of the more important catagories on my reviewing scale, yet it is weighted the same as the rest. I'm proud of this catagory, as this is one that no one has ever used before, and I still think no one has used this (except for MrG, who if I remembered correctly used my scale to review Dustball back years ago. Thats one game I've never played before). Design includes the build-up of the game; are the boards designed well? Are there good added features, such as custom battle systems, or bomb defusing programs, or stuff like that? Are the levels well designed, and are there a good variety in terms of characters, weapons, spells, and the like? A lot of times, reviewers would review these two seperatly, and weigh them the same individually as other aspects. I don't care for that, as they aren't all that important. Combined, they can make the difference in the mood and atmosphere of the game, and decides whether a player gets enveloped into the game or just hitting buttons to accomplish a goal. Good music that fits the mood is a prerequisite, and sounds, an aspect heavily ignored by all game makers, and when and how they are used. A fairly simple catagory to critique. Not much to explain here, except how well the story is. Does it progress forward? Is it predictable? Is it cliched? I put a lot less emphasis on story then most other members in the community, but I find a good story makes it worthwhile to progress through the game and realize that you, the player, are making it unfold. The single most underrated catagory in not only toolkit game reviews, but in the Toolkit game creators' minds as well. Not very many people actually consider if their game is fun to play as a game. I have rarely seen games that were fun to play. Very often, Design and Gameplay go hand in hand. Usually if Design is a 4 or 5, Gameplay somewhere up there as well. The aspects of gameplay (variety of weapons, spells, custom battle systems) are directly involved with the actual game. Is the game fun to play? Is it damn tedious, fighting random monsters after monsters that do horrendous damage early on and slow down the immersion process (*cough* the internal battle system *cough*)? Just think fun here. A very minor piece that not very many reviewers have thought of, or if they have, not very much emphasis is placed on it. About 95% of Toolkit games are bug-ridden, and most of the time (if compiled on a highly stable release of trans2) its the programmer's fault. Look for typos in dialouges, and for RPGCode errors. Another thing is graphical errors, yet most of the time it is out of the creator's control. - Reviewer's Choice Award (or Absofuckinglutly Awesome) - Excellent - Good Above Average - Okay - Tolerable - Absolute Shit Obviously, I have ripped those from PCGamer's scale, but it works. To figure out the percentage, add up all the values in each of the catagories, and divide it by 30. Fairly simple, though you could probably find another, more original way. Many reviewers, when considering a scores, are thinking of the traditional reviewing method (found in classrooms across America): - Excellent - Above Average - Okay - Tolerable - Absolute Shit Obviously, compared to my scale, these are weighted towards the bottom of the list, so if a game is merely okay, they get something in a 70% range. You can't compare the two together. In school, in order to pass, you have to of learned the stuff you were taught, so if you remember half the stuff, you didn't accomplish what you had to do, so you fail and must take the class again. You didn't really 'fail' so to speak, but you did not do as you were supposed to, which is to learn, so you must stay to learn some more. Fairly simple philosophy. Knowing 3/4th of what you learned isn't all that great, but its a fairly basic foundation to use; hence, the C average. Anyway, with games, it doesn't work like that. You have one out of a hundred that your game could end up. Thats a wide range, and if all your games that you review end up in the top 30, what the hell defines a game down in the bottom 70? I've seen crappy games get scores in the top 30, so I would not want to see a game get, say, a 50%. Then lets establish the scale. With my reviewing methods, the lowest possible score is a 20% (thats 1 in all 6 catagories). So a game with, say, horribly cheezy original tiles (worse then ripped RM2k tiles), boards with random solid tiles thrown around, background music that resembles rap, a story involving a kid sent on a quest to save a princess, which wouldn't work, as that'd be statuatory rape, having a game where you'd die in your first battle (with a horrendously bad looking badguy in the internal battle system), and a bug that prevents you from doing anything (even accessing the menu), gets that score (wow, thats a long run-on). Now lets define the best game as 100%; we'll make this D&C 1.1 (I know nothing about other, newer, and possibly better games, so lets just go from there). Now, my biggest flaw in that game was poor board design, not enough gameplay elements, and somewhat cheezy sprites and character graphics (ah hell), but it still did quite well in all catagories, managing a 77%, which is 'Good', as it was a good game. Now comes a game with supurb board design (X-Mas Blobs), fun gameplay (Never-Ending Scroll), and great sprite graphics (Dustball, though I've never played it). What does it get, a 124%? If extra credit is decided, then the validity and the trust towards your scale is gone. The scale needs to be regimental, and a deciding factor in what makes people want to play it. There has not been one game that deserved an 80% or higher (yeah, I have it to X-Mas Blobs, but I rounded down to 'Good' instead of 'Excellent', as I thought that was too high). Basically, score the game according to what you think can be done to a game designed with the Toolkit. Always leave plenty of room for those games; full, complete games, with stunning gameplay and wonderful aspects and design. They will come, and leave room for those games. But always consider what greatness can be achieved, and set that to 100%. Now you have your maximum, and your minimum. Figure out where the game fits in that arena. Always make sure, even for awesome games, that there is some room, as you'll never know. Ah, yes, the golden globe in TK game making. I like the story! 88%. Story was about some guy who worked for the army, then there was a plot twist, and the guy has to attack the evil guy which took over army. Agh! Yes, cliched stories can be cool, actually. Never-Ending Scroll and Dark Age, though fairly cliched, were written so well, you wanted to keep going, and were immersed in the tale, even knowing what was definitly going to happen next. But that is difficult to pull off, so always go for original tales. Its hard to go for original stories, as only 30-so stories actually exist. But in this case, a non-anime influenced story is original to me, and non-fairy tale also. For example, if the bad guys win (or you play the bad guys throughout, not even changing roles during the over and badly used 'plot twist'), if there is no plot twist period, and you know the enemy from the start (and the reasons for fighting him/her are unique, such as being accidental or something); those are all cool ideas I'd love to see creators try and pull off. There are too many plot tutorials, and the community is overflushed with such. Many of them tell you how to create a good cliched story. Don't! Just come up with something random, that no one would really ever expect any one to do. Just make sure if your game is serious, its not too random (like Pink Penguins appearing if Santa's factory is discovered, which, by the way, is in a story I wrote once). You'll get high scores from me at least! Remember, fun! Was the game ever boring? Did you feel yourself wanting to get through that damn cave through the mountains, trying to get on with the story? Yeah, I know, all the time. Most Toolkit games are stories with long, boring caves between one plot point and the next. The only will to go on is to know what happened next. If its gonna be plot driven, make sure that the experience there is fun. Cyrus did an excellent job at that with Chapter 1.1 of Deceit and Conquest. Chapter 1 had a great story, but the game had no variety, not freedom, and too damn hard. Cyrus added great gameplay elements with 1.1, and I really wished he continued making that game, as he stopped because of all the harsh criticsm (maybe because of me, but I was actually wanting him to finish, because I knew he'd get better). Make sure those damn caves are shorter, or not so much in the way of board-filling rooms. Remember, level design, which all goes back to Design. The battles better be good. In almost all Toolkit games, they are relatively difficult from the beginning, which makes no sense, as how much higher does the creator go as he progresses on the game? This is similar to the Reviewing Scale concept. See where you want to end first, and see what you should have in the beginning to make it easy but fun, and progress that way. Mostly the internal battle engines' fault, as because of its nature it is hard to make easy enemies easy without being ridiculously easy. Side-quests are always cool, allowing you to get treasures and free stuff. But don't make optional stuff so damn hard, that if you can beat it, you'll know you can beat the end. Just have hidden stuff and secret quests throughout the game, making the player want to find the objectives, and have fun doing it. I can't really go off on what you should have in your game. Its really all up to you. Just make sure it isn't all hard, boring fighting that goes on and on through boring levels. That's really all you need to go off on. Like I said, fun! Typos in text will be your most common bug you'd encounter. That's only a 4, which can be fairly high, especially if the game isn't on excellent level of quality. This can be the redeeming factor in most games, as a bug ridden game can sometimes make a game unplayable, and usually people will post playable games. The main reason why not very many of my reviewed games got below a 50% was because they were fairly debugged, and got quite a few points in this catagory. Graphical glitches need to be addressed, and some penalties need to be put on the game, even if it is the engine's fault, and not the creators. Firstly, you'll never know exactly whose fault it was, unless it was an actual bug that has been discovered and you know about it (of course, who will know all the known bugs in all the different versions that games were made for). There should be no real need to go below a 3, unless there are plenty of RPGCode syntax errors, that prevents dialouges, warp programs, and other important things from happening. Depending on the importance of the command in error to the actual game, should be the amount taken off from the 5. Unlike all the other scores, this starts at 5, and goes down (at least that's how I think of it as).

I' m sorry, I went on to long about the final percentage without further enforcing what makes up the final percentage; the individual scores. However, as you read through this section, I want you to think about the final percentage, but make that a goal for your scores. Make your scores, add them up, and see where you get, but if the percentage is too high or low, adjust one or two values in the catagories. But still, don't go for the 70% range, and force your scores to go into that range, because it won't be accurate. Anyway, on with graphics. I'm huge on the arts, and it bugs me to see ripped graphics. But this is a mixed bag arena. My biggest pet-peeve is the use of the RPGMaker tilesets, as those are the ones everybody uses. If they pull off a good selection of graphics that is arranged in a way that is pleasing to the eye, give the score a 3, at most. Usually, RPGMaker tiled games only get a 2 in this catagory. If there are non-RM2K ripped tiles, then don't be afraid to hit 4, but only as long as they are used supurbly. But never give a 5 to ripped tilesets, as that encourages laziness in the art department. Ripped graphics can be a whole lot better then originals. Have you seen some originals? Jesus. Easy 1 and 2s for most people out there who try original graphics. If you can't do them, then used ripped, please! However, if they can pull off great non-sprite tiles, a 3 or 4 is not out of the question. The hardest thing to get right, though, is sprites. Good looking sprites, alongside nice background tiles, is an easy 5; sounds too easy? No it ain't, as very few have pulled that off. Now, we have the conflict between styles. I gave a 4 to X-Mas Blobs, but a 3 to D&C. The style, mood, atmosphere, and content of the Blobs make it where the cartoony look to the Blobs looks great! It fits the objective, and the style. D&C's score, even though I'd much rather give a 3.5, was rated as such because even though the tiles are much better in terms of detail and time then the Blobs, it wasn't quite enough to satisfy the atmosphere. The background tiles were fine, but the sprites weren't that great (and the cutscenes dragged this score down too). Oh yeah, bitmaps. A whole 'nother catagory. Tiles should be the prime focus, though a vast assortment of images are always used too, if not in cutscenes or profile pics, then as buttons or menu backgrounds. In D&C's case, I just plain don't like the way the characters are drawn in the cutscenes. Yeah, it'd be perfect for a different setting, like a comic strip or something (a la Blobs), but for the gritty, dark, realistic approach that Cyrus offered, it didn't float my canoe. Really, all graphics are is style, and if they fit the style or not. For menu buttons, title screens, and the like, go read my Logo and Title Screen tutorial. That should present to you the philosophies of those images. Yeah yeah yeah, my baby. I love this, and I love great design over great gameplay when it comes to Toolkit games, as great design represents a high level of effort (not the type of effort like trying your hardest to place last at a track meet, but trying hard to finish in the top 4 in the first heat, whereas a top athlete not trying hard would get 6th). The bulk of this score is decided to board design. My is bad board design. I hate vast open spaces, and I hate nothing in a room. I like house and room sizes that fit with what is going on, and plenty of tiles and stuff to make it look cool and real. Refer to my Board Design Tutorial for more philisophical insight. Also, other aspects of design that assist gameplay. Are there are variety of spells that invoke the use of strategy (something rare in Toolkit games)? Cure spells, attack spells, group attack spells, confuse spells, and teleportation spells (always add a nice touch), all provide a solid design and hopefully a gaming experience. Different weapons, such weapons that deal more damage but are two-handed; those decide what you're willing to sacrifice to achieve the strategy you hope to deliver. Basically, the point here, is variety, and not variety for the sake of variety, but variety for the sake of good gameplay. Custom stuff always helps. Custom menus, non-combat gameplay elements (like a race, or defusing bombs, or other things), and best of all, custom combat engines, all contribute greatly to this score. If board design and trivial varieties form the base of design, let these be the positive modifiers. Anygame with a decent, fun, bugless battle engine deserves at least a 3 in design, even if the boards are absolute hell. But if there boards are decent, and a good variety of items can be used in that battle engine, you got yourself a 5 here. 5 can be achieved with great boards, non-combat gaming expierences, and custom miscellanious stuff like message windows and menus. This all adds to the gaming nirvana, and total immersion factor, into your unique game. Not all that complex here. Ripped music had better be good. No over-used FF4/6/7 music, or Zelda music. That stuff gets on my nerves eventually. Ripped music that fits the mood nicely, is not annoying and overwhelming, and comes from a not so much used source is always a plus, and can fortify a 3, with additional bonuses added on through sound. Never should a game with ripped FF/Zelda music score a 5, for the same reasons as for graphics. Original music is damn hard, as you can't really just teach yourself music theory without having some background (I never took theory classes, but from my 6 years of piano playing, it wasn't hard at all to write my first song, which doesn't even sound that bad). Original music always kicks ass, and I like it. Sounds are rare in Toolkit games. The sound portion of this catagory gets help from good, unobnoxious sound effects in battles, but mainly, for clicking buttons and stuff, that will help tremendously. Even Windows default sounds are perfect for clicking buttons and other minor trivial things like that. There should never be a 5 without sounds. DON'T

Conclusion
Well, thats it. Just follow everything, and write good, honest reviews. Two key things unmentioned to think about; don't be nice. Be honest. If you're nice, all we have will be 80%+ games, and that sucks. Be completly honest, and have a good means to back up your scale on. And the other things; don't give credit because of effort. As I said, a person can give all the effort in the world, but only achieve 12th place in the 100 meter dash. Give credit based off of the final product; who gives a damn how the creator made it (except if he ripped everything), just that he made it, and it looks good.
