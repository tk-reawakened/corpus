Written by W.L.Burgett (figement68@hotmail.com) BASIC SPRITE CREATION : TUTORIAL 1
*Edited by Vampz for Toolkit Zone*
Note: Click to download the templates needed for this tutorial and extract them to the tilesets folder. If you are viewing this file offline, then the tilesets should be included in the download, simply copy & paste them to the tilesets folder. [here](fileman.php?id=3&file=82)
So you need some help with creating original sprites for your “RPG toolkit” game? There are a number of ways to do so. If you are attempting to find readymade sprites, this tutorial is not for you. To find out more about where to obtain readymade sprites, go to the “Twisted Karma” web site. But if you are wanting to learn more about creating your own high quality, one of a kind sprites, designed specifically for your own game, then this tutorial will supply you with everything that you will need. So get the caffeine, kick your shoes off and let’s get started!
**Introduction**
First things first. I am assuming that since you are reading this, that you already have a version of the RPG Toolkit. If not, then get one now at the official home, www.rpgtoolkit.com. At the time of writing, the most recent version of the RPG Toolkit is 2.17. And it will be the tile editor contained within version 2.17 that we will be working with. The artistic concepts covered in this tutorial will apply to creating “sprites” or “icons” using whatever editor you may be using, but the templates and tile samples will only work with the RPG toolkit.
It’s time to crank up the Toolkit. I would suggest that you make the appropriate storage space for the templates and sprite samples contained with this tutorial. The simplest is to create a blank database in the Toolkit under the main game menu and name it “Sprite Tutorial” or something like that, however, if you don't you want to do it is fine as long as the files will be able to be loaded into the tile editor and also so that you will have a compatible file to store the sprites you create in.
Open the tile editor, and open the “tmplt1.tst”. Choose the first tile in the set. This is the ‘Basic Human Form’ template referred to hereafter as BHF template. All tiles created in this tutorial are 32x32. Now we are ready to begin.
![](fileman/images/34.jpg)
Your screen should now look like this. Yeah, it doesn’t look inspiring but it is very functional. For this tutorial I am going to construct a small sprite of myself. The first thing is “What colors are we going to use?” The first color to consider is the skin tone. This will depend on what type of life form your sprite is going to represent. A goblin might have green skin, or a demon might have red flesh, but for the sprite of itself is a human of Anglo-Saxon origins, so a light orange-pink will best represent my flesh tones.
**Three Tone Method**
There are a few ways to select a color in the Toolkit. The first is to utilize the color bar at the bottom of the tile editor. But if you are going to be using a color more than once, it is better to use the color selection palette. Here you can create custom colors and load them onto the temporary custom color palette so that you don’t have to waste time matching the color up on the color bar. This is the method that I most often use, and it is the one that I would recommend. The other is to use RGB values. This method is just as accurate but again a little more time consuming.
When choosing your colors, you should choose a minimum of three different values for each color that you pick out. In other words if you choose the color green, also pick out a green tone that is slightly lighter than the color that you choose, and pick another green tone that is slightly darker than the original green that you chose. This is so that we can give the sprite the illusion of depth and add highlights. Illusion is the key, especially when working with very small items such as sprites where you are not going to be able to use a lot of detail. But with the careful placement of color when creating our sprites we can fool the eye when you see the finish sprite at it's normal size into registering details that aren’t truly there.
![](fileman/images/35.jpg)
**Applying The Colors**
Once you have chosen the skin tone colors, load the lightest color, that is the color that is lighter than the one that you originally picked out, and completely fill in the fleshy areas of your sprite.
![](fileman/images/36.jpg)
After you have something that looks like this, then load a darker color (I usually choose black, but it can vary as long as it is darker than the rest if your sprite) and trace the light green outline on the sprite. Now you should have something that looks like this:
![](fileman/images/37.jpg)
Now we come to the most important part to creating a well designed sprite. It is time to apply the different tones or shading of colors. It is a simple procedure, but you would not believe how many people over-look this method and end up with bland, flat and unappealing sprite. Load the color that you originally chose. We are going to apply colors in layers that will give the illusion of shadow and depth. To create the illusion of shadow we must first envision from where this virtual light source is originating. In general for RPG board sprites, it is always best to assume that the light source is coming from above. There are exceptions to this such as torches in caves or camp fires, and by creating multiple instances of the same character in your games you can get very complicated with changing the lighting or appearance, such as clothing and weapons carried, of a character within the game. But for now we are going to stick to the general assumption that the light source is coming from above for this simple sprite. It is best to envision this source as being directly above rather than to the left or right, this way the shadows and shading are straight vertically, that is up and down. The reason for this will become evident as we proceed.
Begin at the jaw line and fill in a pixel area between 2-4 spreading outward. Also fill the ear area and the low parts of the hands. For demonstration purposes I am going to go ahead and apply this technique to the torso as well, but unless your sprite is going to be skyclad (nude) you can skip the parts that will later be covered with clothing.
![](fileman/images/38.jpg)
Now you should have a sprite that looks something like this. Next load up the darkest skin tone that you chose and repeat the step but this time only fill in a pixel space of 1-2. This is also when you might want to change the eye color and add a dark color to the two pixels that will represent the lips or mouth.
You should have a sprite that looks similar to the one in the next image:
![](fileman/images/39.jpg)
Continue to color in pixels to represent clothing and hair using the three tones principle. As you do so try to imagine the shapes of things, the contours of the fabrics, the shape of the face, etc. This is especially effective when it comes to the hair. Repeat this process and dress your sprite.
![](fileman/images/40.jpg)

**Creating Movement**
But a single sprite just standing there isn't of much use now is it? Now we must create additional tiles that when combined in an animation will give your sprite some life. First save your finished sprite. Then grab your eraser and (don't freak out) begin erasing the left leg, starting at the bottom of the foot and working your' way up to where the knee would be. Then go to the right arm and knock off about 6-8 pixels from the bottom there.
Now you have a messed up looking sprite that resembles this:
![](fileman/images/43.jpg)
Don't be frightened. Take the dropper tool that will load the colors that you have used in your sprite, and redraw the hand on the shorter arm, then redraw the foot on the shorter leg but this time make it one pixel wider. Save the finished tile in the same folder as you did the first one.
Now if you kept your shading relatively up and down you can just click on the tool button at the top of the tile editor window, then click on flip. If you angled your' shading and shadows you will have to redraw them now to make sure that your' sprite appears to move smoothly and realistically when animated. This will not be a problem if you kept your shading and shadows centered earlier. You should now have a set of tiles that depict your sprite as walking forward.
Simply repeat the process using the three-tone method on the side view and the back view. Don't forget to switch the actual positioning of the legs in the side views!
I included additional templates for these views with the tileset. Feel free to use these templates as many times for whatever you need, they are useful guides for aspect ratio and proportioning when creating sprites.
![](fileman/images/44.jpg)
Take time to add additional details to your sprites, such as extra attire and weapons etc. You may also want to add a ground shadow to your sprite added realism and depth.
