### obliq · 2007-01-23 21:04 · #K374716

Jiminy snickers, seafroggys, you have a real issue taking critique. When the tough start pushing, you fold like a folding chair.
1) this is not the first outburst I've seen from you when being critiqued, and 2) you give up too easily.
I'm going to give it to you straight. This is likely the most important advice I have ever given anyone on here.
If you want to right a story about a really cool, powerful, state-of-the-art warship, that's great. And you're right about one thing: no one is really going to want to read about it (unless they are rabid Star Trek fans who gobble up technical manuals like they're candy).
This is where my philosophy of science fiction comes into play. It's not about the toys; it's about the people. And people staff the warship and they have lives beyond their jobs and their duties. Bad relationships, self-doubt, low self-esteem, addiction, so on. A lot of times these foibles can interfere with their duties and put the vessel and the crew at risk. A bit clichéd, but a place to start.
Want to talk about the technical details of the warship? Then the characters in the story have to interact with it in some way.
Your story is about a pending war, or in scale of the moment, a pending battle. A new powerful warship is being dispatched to the battlefield. How do you get technical about it? Although state-of-the-art, there are always little glitches that may not be a big deal on the surface but might cause a major failure at a critical moment, like the gunner's console locks up during a firefight (damn Windows!). That would suck.
There might be people on the ship who are concerned that events leading up to the battle are worth discussing, but most people are going to be concerned about the moment like the green ensign seeing combat for the first time and afraid he will never see his family again or the leutenant who's light-years away from home awaiting word of the birth of his first child, and upset that he can't be there. To add insult to injury, he won't get it because the ship is operating under radio silence. These are the sorts of things that are going to weigh on the minds of poeple.
Likewise, what are the people on the other side of this conflict going through? Someone might be having issues with the inevitability of their approaching demise. How does someone deal with the idea that they are going to die, and there's nothing they can do to stop it? You could also have the commander of this force have a breakdown because he can't raise the morale of his troops. After all they are facing the prospect of the most powerful warship raining feiry destruction down on them. But can they win against overwhelming odds?
Perhaps a valiant and resourceful captain in the enemy fleet discovers the Achilles Heel of the new-fangled warship and wrestles with the idea of sacrificing himself and what's left of his ship and crew to bring it down in one final blaze winning the battle for his side. And don't forget to think of all the things that could go wrong in that process.
Anyway, my final thought is this: you have to learn to view critique as an opportunity to improve on what you've done and not as an assault on your delusions of granduer.
With that said, you can improve what you have, toss it out with yesterday's garbage and move on to something else, or start the idea over from scratch.
