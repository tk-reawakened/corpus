### comfox · 2004-08-08 02:45 · #B71530

I haven't yet seen a reply that successfully adresses the issue in your favour, my friends... Let's go through the most convincing-sounding points, shall we?

**Quote:**

> Evil is just the absence of Goodness. Therefor, an Omnipotent Purely Good God can create the world such as ours, and evil can still exist... Due to free will, we have the choice of being separate from God, or separate from 'Goodness', therefor God's choice of freewill [which isn't evil] let us choose to become evil. God didn't create evil, it is merely an absence of him. Much like darkness..
>

But what is absence? If you tell me that evil is only the absence of good, I can accept that as a logical idea. But if you tell me that an omnipotent and purely good being created everything- I'm going to have to disagree. You see, the claim of omnipotence is really what gets you here; God prefers good to evil (henceforth a "lack of good"). God is omnipotent. THEREFORE: God, being bound by no laws of logic or indeed of any kind, is entirely capable of creating a world in which there is no "absence" of good. This is where my argument differs with the professor's. Just as duality is not a natural law that God must be confined to, your idea of non-duality (or whatever you prefer to call it) is not a natural law that God must be confined to. He has no obligation to allow something not to exist in certain situations; he has no obligation to leave the scale of its existence one-dimensional at all, as it is in both the case of duality and your idea. Why follow a number LINE? There is no concievable reason for an allpowerful being to create something it abhors; and quarreling about the criteria for "something" is pointless. An allpowerful being has no concievable reason for creating something that it abhors or for creating an absence of something it loves. Your argument disproves the common anti-god arguments based on duality, but mine is much more fundamental than those. As long is God is omnipotent, beyond the all laws both physical and conceptual, nothing will exist which God dislikes and nothing will not exist that God likes. Think about it.

**Quote:**

> We must remember that we as humans do not define what is good and evil... God defines what is good and evil. Thus, us calling God good, is actually redundant. God is the rock on which those two terms are built.
>

And as creations of God, with a god-given concience, it should not be possible for our sense of good and evil to become terribly skewed from the true one. If you don't get the belief that God is good from your own self, where do you get it? Either you are told it is true and find yourself in agreement, or someone told you it was true and you just figured it must be. I strongly doubt the latter is what happened- so you must be in agreement. In any case it is irrelevant, because the entire premise of my argument is that God IS good, a statment with which you agree. Both you and God (according to the Bible, Koran, and all other monotheistic texts I have knowledge of) believe that there is evil in God's creation but not in God. Explain that, if you can, in philosophical terms. Here is the problem:
God is like a light. Light is good, God is a source of light (and the light itself). Shine a lamp on something sometime; you'll note portions of darkness on the object/area being lit. Shadows; evil. They form because of two things:
a) The lamp is only shining on them from one position.
b) The lamp has only a finite amount of brightness.
God is omnipresent, he shines from all positions. God is omnipotent, he shines with infinite strength. Therefore a God-lamp produces no shadows. Therefore the PRESENCE of shadows (evil) in our world is proof that the lamp is either not shining at full power or not shining from all directions- and since God is good and therefore WANTS to shine at full power and all directions, the only possible explanation is a defect in the lamp, a weakness in God. Either he doesn't WANT to shine everywhere (he wants evil to exist, simply because he likes it there) or he CAN'T shine everywhere (he is unable to eradicate evil- which means he cannot be omnipotent).

**Quote:**

> "If God is willing, but not able, to destroy evil, then he is impotent,
> If God is able, but not willing, to destroy evil, then he is malevolent,
> If God is both able and willing, why then is there evil?"
>

Exactly.

**Quote:**

> Omnipotent means all powerful. Look it up. It has nothing to do with God being everywhere.
>

Omnipotent does not mean omnipresent (although Christianity does say God is omnipresent, I left this out of my argument because all of its effects are implied by omnipotence). However, omnipotence means that God has the ABILITY to be omnipresent or to effect things as if he were omnipresent- and if he dislikes evil and needs to be omnipresent to get rid of it he will be so. Understandable?

**Quote:**

> The ability to CHOOSE for ourselves allows there to be an absense of good and thus evil.
>

Um... no, actually. The ability to choose does not imply that certain choices must exist. The fact that I have free will does not necessarily mean that I have the ability, if I wish, to break the laws of physics and fly away to the moon in only my boxers and socks. It would only give me that ability if God created that possibility, deliberitely, in the first place. God had to create the possibility of turning against him for it to be a choosable choice. In fact he had to create the concept of turning against him for us to even talk about it or think about it. Now why would that choice exist if God didn't have to make it and if it does no good for anyone (since everyone who chooses it will apparently be tossed into a pit of burning sulfur and eternal pain...)? Being who he is, he knows if the choice's existence is of any use to anyone. And it is not. Not even to him, since by virtue of his omnipotence he can accomplish his desires without tools which hurt others. Again, the ability to choose does not imply that certain choices must exist.

**Quote:**

> Do not think that man's logic and understanding could possibly grasp the truth of anything. Let alone God.
>

We don't need the ability to understand something in its entirety in order to prove it impossible. You don't need to grasp all the laws of geometry to prove a plane triangle of a total of 500 degrees impossible. You only need to know enough to get to the flaw. Same way with God. I don't need to understand the entire blueprint of the universe, the entire nature of infinity and truth and life, in order to prove an omnipotent and purely good God impossible. I need to understand some very very basic things. That's all.
