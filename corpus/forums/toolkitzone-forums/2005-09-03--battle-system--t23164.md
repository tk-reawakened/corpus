### Marz · 2005-09-03 21:40 · #B253549

A turn based battle system has three main parts:
First part: The Battle Loop (seen in all battle systems)
To strip it down, this basically keeps the battle going until the player or the enemy have <=0 health. Once this happens, the battle should end.
Second Part: Player's Turn
What does the player do on his turn? The player usually picks what he gets to do, so in this part you have the player selecting something like "Attack", and having the method carried out.
After the player does his thing, check the Enemy's HP to see if it's dead or not. If so, end the loop.
Third Part: Enemy's Turn
What *should* the enemy do? The player doesn't get to pick what the enemy does in the battle, so this is where you programming Artificial Intelligence comes in. Whether it's as simple as your enemy always just attacking the player, or having your enemy checking various things (whether it should heal itself, whether it should attack another party member, etc), this can be as complicated or as simple as you like.
After the enemy does what it has to do, check the Player's HP to see if he's dead or not. If so, end the loop.
Now return to the beginning of the loop, where it's the players turn. There, easy as pie. Smile

### p90m16mp5a4 · 2005-09-05 22:13 · #B254467

savescreen is inefficient, as any handrawn graphics to the screen will be erased once the battle starts. Also, putting 'loops' in battles can be thought as "professional", when in fact, its much easier to ditch the loops, and is almost just as readable without. By 'ending' the game, and going to a fight, this gives you the freedom for turn-based fest, you dont need to structure your battle commands in loops, have one program do the menus, then shoot it to the next program, then shoot to the next. If you get caught up in loops, while its not messy, you will most likely lose structure if you cant mentally remember all the things you have to keep track of.
