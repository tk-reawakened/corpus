# Let It Come

### Lisha · 2006-05-06 14:06 · #B318824

I just felt like writing a short, emotional story, so here it is. Rather depressing, though. C&C appreciated.

**Quote:**

> There are some things that you never want to think about.
> There are some things that you want to shut out leave them, want to squeeze your eyes just and then open them, and find that it was just a bad dream.
> But you cant, because they arent.
> Thats why Im here now, sitting in the guidance counselors office at school. Because I want to shut things out, but I cant. Because its making me so unhappy.
> She says that she cares. She says that it matters if Im happy or not. But thats a lie, obviously. Well, okay, so maybe half a lie. She just looks at me and thinks, Oh, how sad, what a sad, sad, girl. But she doesnt really care. Not really.
> You cant force me to be happy, you know, I say to her bluntly. Its impossible.
> She sighs and rises from her seat, goes to the window and peers out of it. She turns to me. Im not trying to force you to be happy, as you put it. Im just trying to help you be happy. Her expression so clearly cries, Im trying to help, cant you see that! Just trying to help!
> I know shes trying to help, but trying to help doesnt solve problems the way she seems to think it does. I tried to help, too, but that didnt change much, did it? I still wasnt good enough. Still wasnt good enough to make Mom happier.
> I look up at her, expressionless. Youre not helping.
> She sighs again, and sits back down. She does this when shes getting frustrated with me. I know. Shes frustrated with me a lot.
> You forgot, didnt you. The day after it happened, she says. Im slightly taken aback. Usually we dont go this route. This is an unexpected turn in the conversation, one that gives me a sharp stab of remembrance before I quickly shut the door on it.
> I dont answer, because Im afraid the memory will begin to push against the door if I do. I silently plead for her to stop, to drop the subject.
> She doesnt.
> You came to school wondering where your mom was, right? And you mentioned it to your friend Hannahs her name, I think. Then Hannah told you, and you got mad at her. You got very mad at her, didnt you, Katie?
> The door rattled, and I push against it harder. I dont answer.
> You screamed that she was lying. I could hear you from here. But you knew, didnt you? If you really thought that it wasnt true, then you would have waved it off so much more lightly. Am I right?
> I feel the door begin to splinter, but I still resist it. But still, after a moment of hesitation, I give a small nod.
> She leans forward, so her face is closer to mine. It wasnt your fault, Katie. She was sick. Mentally ill. It isnt your fault that she hung herself. Not even a little.
> No I still think, resisting against the breaking door. No
> Just let the pain come, she says. Just let it come. Youll feel so much better.
> I dont understand how pain can make me feel better. But before I know what Im doing, I raise my eyes at look into the guidance counselors face.
> And then I step away from the door, and let it fly open.
> Moms gone. Shes dead. Hung herself last week, were the first words that entered my mind. She committed suicide. But it isnt my fault.
> It isnt my fault.
> Then I looked down, and saw a small, round dark spot on my sleeve. I brushed my fingers against it. A tear.
> It was a relief.
