# Saphyra Chapter 1 (new version)

### Lisha · 2006-05-29 01:59 · #B323464

Yep, here it is. Chapter one. Kinda short, but *shrug* to that I guess. I realize that in the very beginning I don't set the scene very well, and I'm not describing my characters, but thankfully this was pointed out by my dad when I showed him the first page and a half and I tried by best to fix it from there on. I think this format works a lot better for the story than the journal entries did. And I made Saphyra a couple of years younger (seventeen) because, another thing my dad pointed out, twenty-one really isn't all that young to go join the army. This also means that Hanev is now supposed to be about 21 himself, since I didn't want too big of an age gap between him and Saphyra. Besides what I meantioned at the beginning of my ramblings about not setting the scene well at the start of the chapter, shred away please (but not TOO harshly Wink ).

**Quote:**

> Saphyra wanted to tear the letter up when she first held it in her hands. Even before she opened it, she knew what it was.
> Mother had looked at it with her lips pushed together and her eyes wide in that half-worried, half-angry expression of hers. She tossed it down onto the table viciously. Already! she spat. Already! They are crazy, for calling you to fight. Absolutely crazy.
> We all knew it was coming, Lianne, Saphyras younger sister, said with her eyes unblinking and round. It had to happen sooner or later. She cast a glance at Saphyra, then quickly looked away as if she was afraid of eye contact.
> You should have been more careful, Saphyra, argued Mother. You should have tried to hide it more. You should have been more careful! She acted angry, but her voice shook. Her fury was a shield designed to hide her fright.
> I was as careful as I could possibly be, Saphyra answered. She pulled the letter over the old, worn wood of the table, staring down at it as she spoke. I only used magic at night, when no one watched. I never bought it, only dug it up from the ground. I never spoke of it with anyone but this family and Daehn. I was as careful as I could be. Her own voice was very quiet, barely audible. She didnt want to go. She was terrified.
> Mother reached across the table and snatched the letter away from Saphyra fiercely. She held it up and read it once more, as if she thought she hadnt read correctly the first time, then again thrust it down onto the table. The sound of her palm hitting the table rang out in an otherwise silent room.
> Damn war! she cried, her voice rising violently. Isnt ten years enough? Isnt that long enough to fight with anyone? What are they even fighting over? Why do they need magicians? Just just tell them that you wont go!
> She has to go, Father said simply, speaking for the first time. There was a note of panic in his voice, even though at first it sounded composed and calm. She has no choice.
> Do you know what happens to people in those wars?! Mother was beginning to sound almost hysterical. Saphyra thought she saw a small wet spot on her cheek. Dont you realize? Didnt you see what Venik Reninea had looked like when he came back, before he died, - she forced the word died out with intense forcefulness - a week later? Didnt any of you see him?
> Saphyra struggled to keep her composure, carefully examining the patterns in the wood of the table. She had seen Venik Reninea. And it wasnt a sight she would like to see again.
> All she could think at that moment, listening to her mother go on, was, That could be me, that could be me
> Mother went on. She screamed about how the war was pointless, useless, that nobody even knew what they were fighting for, that it was long overdue, that it was unneeded and uncalled for and unacceptable. She went on about all of the things that happened to people in those wars, all of the horrible things, and asked why, when there were thousands of strong men in the country, it had to be her seventeen-year-old daughter that had to fight. All of these things she shouted out chaotically, impulsively, her words laced with the worst kind of fury she had ever felt in her life.
> And as she raved and ranted and fumed, Saphyra sat there, in the little rickety wooden chair, staring down at the table.
> Trying not to cry.
> Then, as Father carefully slipped between Mothers shouts and they began to argue, and Saphyra felt that she might not be able to take any more of this, she felt a pair of thin, young arms wrap around her neck in a hug.
> Its alright, Saphyra, she heard Liannes voice whisper softly. Its alright. Youll be fine. Let them argue. Let Mother scream. They just need to get it out of them.
> Saphyra curled her fingers around Liannes arm and just let her hand rest there. Somehow it comforted her.
> * * *
> And then she told Daehn. She met him out in the fields; the most quiet, serene place that either of them knew of. It was her favorite place. She would miss it.
> He had listened patiently as she explained, and then silently read the letter when she handed it to him. Then he lifted his eyes, and looked straight into hers.
> You cant do this, he said weakly. This is just His voice trailed off, and he shook his head.
> She sunk down into the long, brown-green grass. He knelt down next to her, not breaking the sad, persistent stare that she wished she could get away from. I have to, she answered. Her fright showed in her voice. I have to go.
> He glanced at the letter again, tightening his fingers around the edge of it. It seemed almost as if he was angry with it. Do you know how many magicians they must have called to fight? he said. Hundreds. Maybe even thousands. If you - one magician girl - never showed up, they wouldnt notice.
> Of course they would, she argued. They have records. If I didnt go, they would just come back here and chop all of our heads off. Is that what you want?
> He ignored that. His voice rose. It should be me going off to war, not you. You cant. Im warning you, Saphyra, Im warning you! If you go He didnt finish the sentence. He didnt want to.
> Doesnt he realize hes only making this harder for me? she thought desperately. Doesnt he realize that if I had a choice, then I would stay? Why cant he see that? Why cant anybody see that? She wanted to just let out all of these thoughts, just scream them out the way Mother did.
> But out loud, she only said, quietly, I need to go. I need to. Please understand that.
> He grimaced and ran his hand through his blond hair. With Daehn, that was never a good sign. It always meant that he was torn, undecided, struggling.
> What if you dont come back? he asked, sounding distraught. Then he said, angrily, Dammit, what was the matter with them?
> She ignored the first question, because it was a question that she didnt even want to ask herself. Carefully she stepped around it, tiptoed to the other question instead. They need some good magicians, she said, forcing her voice to be even. They need us for the war.
> I dont care about the war, do you? He snapped a dried blade of grass between his fingers. I dont even know what this war is over. No one does. They cant blame you for not wanting to fight for something you dont even know about. They cant blame you if you say no.
> They shouldnt, but they will. Her voice had a strong air of finality to it. They were the hands that closed the book, that locked the door. They were concluding.
> He couldnt find a way to answer to them.
> Now, as Saphyra rode in a cab along the worn, dirt path to the Quefan Military Base, they echoed in her head. The goodbyes were still fresh in her mind. There had been hugs, kisses, tears
> And promises. So many promises.
> Promise me that youll be careful.
> Promise to write whenever you can.
> Promise that you wont forget about me.
> Promise to keep your chin up.
> To all of these she just nodded and said seriously, I promise, I promise. and meaning it. But then there was one thing that she couldnt promise, even though it was the one she wanted to the most.
> Promise youll come home. Just promise that youll come home.
> To that she had just looked down and shook her head, and said softly, Ill try to.
> And that was why, as she sat in the back of the cart thinking about those words, They should, but they wont, a mix of anger and grief flamed through her body.
> Why shouldnt they? she thought angrily, resting her chin on her knees like she hadnt done since she was little. Why shouldnt they? They are the reason why I cant make that promise. They are the reason why I had to make any promises at all. Why should I have to fight for a country I have no patriotism for? Fight in a war that I dont even know anything about?
> Her thoughts were interrupted by the gruff voice of the driver, an broad-shouldered man in his fifties. He seemed like the kind of man who had seen a lot of work in his life, the kind of man that didnt stand for nonsense. Going to join the army? he asked. His voice seemed strangely cracked.
> Saphyra turned to face him, even though he was still facing away from her. Yes, she said, sounding disheartened. She was disheartened.
> Sons in the army, the driver went on. The way he said it made it sound like a short, husky bark, as if he wanted to get the words out as quickly as possible. Been there for a year now.
> Oh. Saphyra wasnt sure how to reply. She said, weakly, Whats his name? Ill look for him.
> The driver gave a wry, humorless laugh. Almost as if he was mocking himself. Dont think youll see him, he answered. His voice seemed even more cracked. He wrote every week for three months. Then his letters stopped. He took one leathery, callused hand from the reins and snapped his fingers. Just like that. And he never wrote again. The last sentence was said bitterly, spiked with only a little bit of sadness.
> D-didnt you get a letter from the military? Saphyra asked awkwardly, fear squeezing her stomach harder now that it had been awakened again. Saying thatthat
> She couldnt find a way to say it.
> Nope. The sadness was gone from the drivers voice, replaced with overly-gruff bluntness. But still it was clear the bluntness was only there to cover up the sadness, and still the sadness shone through.
> We never got any letter, continued the driver. No letters. Not from him, not from the military. We just never heard from him again.
> Silence.
> Then the driver abruptly resumed, voice suddenly angry, Hed dead now, of course. We dont need no letter to tell us that. Why would he stop writing, all of the sudden? Hed dead, thats why. And the damn country doesnt care enough to just write a letter.
> From behind him I saw his balding head shake back and forth slowly. If this country doesnt change its ways fast, well have a revolt soon. No one has any patriotism anymore. No one likes this country.
> Saphyra sighed. I cant argue with that, she said softly. She made no effort to hide the fear in her voice.
> There was a brief silence, then the driver cleared his throat huskily and said with awkward gentleness, Im sorry. I didnt mean to scare you.
> Its alright. Then she changed the subject. How will the trip go?
> The driver seemed more than relieved to escape the topic. If all goes as planned, well get to the base in about three days. We can stop in a town to rest each night, and that along should keep us on schedule, because well have to get to the town before we stop traveling, see? he answered with renewed enthusiasm. We got to cross the Floureene River, so well have to go a little out of our way to cross the Hanian Bridge, but well only lose two or three hours if Whin here can move fast. The horses ears flicked back as he heard his name, and his walk got just a little more flamboyant as if he meant to say indignantly, Of course I can move fast!
> Saphyra gave a short laugh. It was needed. The moment it escaped her it let a little of her worry out with it.
> She couldnt see the drivers face, but she could tell that he was smiling just from the way his voice sounded when he spoke. Guess you arent that old, eh Whin?
> Whin bobbed his head up and down and lifted his legs a bit higher.
> The driver turned around to look at her, and he was smiling. Hes showing off for you. Hes a terrible old flirt.
> Saphyra laughed in a girlish, giggly way that usually drives her herself to the point of insanity when she hears it. She could not help herself, though. It was as if she was starved for laughter, and it escaped her without any permission from her.
> The driver turned around again, and when he spoke his voice was suddenly solemn. Youre too young to be going off to this war, he said seriously, but I think, just from talking to you a little bit, that you can make it through this. I would bet anything that you could. Now Whin and I want to be the one to bring you back home, alive and happy, and Im already broke enough so dont make me lose this bet! Towards the end, his voice was almost forceful, but not quite. There was a certain amount of gentleness to it.
> Saphyra bit her lip, holding back tears left over from her goodbyes suddenly awakened, then composed herself and answered equally seriously, I wont. I wont. Ill come back alive.
> And when you come back, the driver went on, voice sunken even lower. My sons name is Nayl Tenranic. Please tell me if you saw him.
> Saphyra couldnt help but smile at that, because even though he sounded disheartened, just that he was saying that proved that he still had hope. Even when his son had stopped writing without any warning, even when they hadnt heard from him in over half of a year, he still believed that his son just might still be alive.
> I will, Saphyra answered, more fiercely than she intended to. I will!
> * * *
> Hanev frowned when he walked into his plain, square, professional-looking room at the Quefan Military Base. There was a letter resting on his bedside table. The envelope was plain, with no writing on it.
> Hanev didnt get letters often.
> Much less ones this sinister looking.
> He held it in his hands for a moment, staring at it, before he slowly opened it. The letter inside it was short and to the point. He read it in less than two minutes.
> Then he let it flutter to the ground, and he sat still, staring at it lie facedown on the floor. He wasnt sure whether to be ecstatic or horrified. Instead, he was half of each.
> He never knew how long he sat there, but it was a long time. His thoughts flitted around in erratic, unpredictable leaps, bouncing from one emotion to another, one decision to the next.
> Then, after a long, long time of thought, he numbly rose and walked to his desk. He sat down, pulled open a drawer, got a sheet of clean paper, pen, and ink. He drew in a deep breath, then slowly let it out as he questioned his decision one last time.
> He hesitated for a moment, then he began to write.
