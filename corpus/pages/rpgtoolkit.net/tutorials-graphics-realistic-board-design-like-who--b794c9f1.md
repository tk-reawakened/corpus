**Overview**
As a reviewer of Toolkit games, I have come across many problems among the design of each of these games.Â There is one problem that is repeated through numerous games, a problem that annoys me and impels me to break the monotony with my own games.Â This plague is the design of boards in games, a plague I want to cure with this tutorial.
When people design and create boards, usually they have an objective to reach, and those boards led to the eventual objective.Â Yes, boards need an objective, but they also require a purpose.Â There are countless boards that led to an objective, but have no soul, no purpose, in the game.Â My first objective is to show you a board that you should not make.Â Can you guess the flaws of this board?
![](vault/images/32_1.jpg)
Lets set some basic principles down.Â Assume, with 32x64 characters, that each tile represents roughly 4 square feet to 1 square meter.Â The above example is the inside of a home.Â Lets count the floor space of the room (including the bed, table, chair, and woodbox).Â This numbers up to 180 tiles, which is at least 720 square feet in size!Â Not only does the size of this room compare to an entryway to a mansion, this room has nothing in it!Â Who realistically would spend the time building a rather big house (for the medieval time period) and only put a bed, table, and woodbox in it?Â I'm not saying 'no' to making such a house, but such a house with nothing in it?Â Lets break this down and create a board large enough for one person to comfortably live with a bed, table, and woodbox, but small enough so that the house is not exceedingly huge.
![](vault/images/32_2.jpg) With the same universal rules in mind, the floor size amounts to 20 square tiles, or at least 80 square feet.Â Quite small, yes, and tiny compared to the poorly designed colassal board in the previous example.Â One person can construct a house relatively inexpensively, put his belongings in it (a table, bed, and woodbox), and still comfortably manuever inside the house.Â Compare the two; what do you think is a better designed board?Â Now, moving on, I'll just show you an example from my game, Ashfall.
This is the main character's house that he shares with![](vault/images/32_3.jpg) his sister, thus some of the parameters must be changed to accomodate for the second person.Â As you can observe, the objects inside the house are all along the perimeter, leaving a rather decent sized gap in the middle of the house.Â That is fine, since the character and is sister are not in poverty, and can afford such spacious areas.Â There are two beds, a chest of drawers, a workdesk, a table with two chairs, a woodbox, a fireplace, and food storage, situated in approximetly 192 square feet of space.Â Those can realistically sustain the two persons for an amount of time.Â Additional items are located in the attic, which is visited during the game.Â A hint: if you forget about a certain item in your board design, you can always include it 'in that attic'.Â Please be careful about exterior design if you do just that.
****Matching Interior and Exterior Houses****
This is a large problem in many home brewed games as well, so when you are designing the preliminary designs for your boards, it is good to think of this section.Â Compare theÂ first example Â in the Overview section with this board below.
Now, wasn't the square footage roughly 720? Lets count the square footage on this board (note that since the wall is two tiles high, two tiles are subtracted from the top of the house to accomadate for the back wall); hmmm, 128 square feet.Â A small difference indeed.Â The problem that exsists is that in many Toolkit games, the outside of houses (or any building in particular) is rather mediocre size, such as the example above. However, the insides are usually filled up all the way on 19x11 boards (like the![](vault/images/32_4.jpg) first example ).Â It is extremely warped, thus, be careful on matching the size of the inside and outside.Â It is okay to be one or two tiles off, no one will really notice.Â So if your house takes up a 19x11 board, make a 19x11 house on your exterior board.Â Large, yes, especially on smaller resolutions, but real, definetly.
As in myÂ third example, my house had an attic.Â Even if it says it has one or not, if, for the reason that you didn't have enough floor space to accomodate for all your household objects, you need one on the outside, as so:

Because of the 45Â° angle of the roof, the attic is quite large, double the height of the actual house in the middle.Â This isn't neccesarily bad at all, since I'm using 45's in Ashfall, but smaller angles are possible, and prevents huge attics from appearing.Â Speaking of height, lets go over the height of rooms.
Your average ceiling in a house is 8 feet above the ground; thats four tiles using the 2x2 foot measurement or three tiles using the 1x1 meter measurement.Â I personally use three, two for the character to stand and an additional tile for head room.Â Those settings are for 64x32 characters, which is better since it matches human porportions much better.Â If you use the traditional 32x32, you either get pudgy characters, like in Traidon, or really skinny characters, like in Deceit and Conquest Pre-Chapter 1.1.Â Thus tiles would be larger in comparison to real life, so heighths of two tiles will suffice.Â Not only is this neccesary in designing exterior housing, but for interiors as well; If you include a backwall, it must match the outside of the house, and vice versa.
****Frontwalls and Backwalls****
In interior boards, I like to call the differents walls names: Frontwalls, Sidewalls, and Backwalls. Frontwalls is the wall closest to your point of view, hence 'front'.Â Backwalls are the walls in the back of the room, and sidewalls, are well, are any wall that doesn't face you.Â There are difference methods of potraying these barriers in not only houses, but caverns as well.
![](vault/images/32_6.jpg)
Never use frontwalls.They cover up two or three tiles in the front of the room.Â Now, most often, in the real world, people put stuff on their 'frontwall', but in video games, there are no items, they are all shoved to the back or sidewalls. To solve two problems, just take the damn thing out.This technique is used often in 3D games, and can be easily implemented into 2D games as well, without too much trouble.Â Below is a comparison between with frontwalls and without.
Assume on a particular board there are two rooms, but instead of being side to side, they are front to back.Â If the back room lacked a front wall, there will be no represenation of a wall, thus the player can assume he can walk directly into the other room. Obviously this cannot be allowed, so there are several choices that are possible.Â You can have two seperate boards altogether, which can be good or bad, but it is your judgement.Â Or, you can have a frontwall, but when designing it, please refer to sidewalls, explained below.![](vault/images/32_7.jpg)
Backwalls are relatively easy to make, just put a big slab of tiles and you have it.Â Sidewalls require a little bit more skill.Â There are a multitude of ways to create sidewalls (and frontwalls should you choose to have a second room behind the front room).Â One common method was used in Final Fantasy 2/4, where all the walls were a whole tile thick.Â I've seen this in quite a few Toolkit games, as a matter of fact, but there are some flaws.Â Using our original proportions, all these walls would be anywhere from two to three feet thick.Â These are fine for military bases or castles, perhaps caves, but not for the standard household.Â For Ashfall, my side walls are roughly 5-10 pixels in width roughly 4-8 inches.Â My house, which is relatively new, is at most ten inches thick, so 5-10 pixels is a rather decent value.Â Even though the FF2 method is fine for fortresses and the like, my method works much better for smaller houses that require thin walls.
****Doors and House Facings****
Have you ever walked into a town, a noticed something very strange; all the buildings are facing the same direction?Â You drive down the road, looking at all the houses facing you, but as you turn onto another avenue, you no longer see any front doors, only sides of houses, all facing the same direction as the houses you encountered before.Â Welcome to the world of RPGs.
In all the 2D top down games I have played, all the doors are facing forward (except for secret doors on the backsides). There is reasoning behind this, since you can't really rotate the camera and reveal doorways on the sides or back.Â However, I have devised a method to remove this glitch and add further realism to your universe.Â Take a look at this pic from Ashfall below.
![](vault/images/32_8.jpg)
Note how both houses do not have doors on their front-facing side?Â So how do you enter?Â Take notice of those black mats on the side of the houses, right off the walkway. Those are doormats, which for obvious reasons are placed right by doors.Â That way, the player can tell where doors are on the side (you might want to explain anyway in the game's readme what those black tiles are), but not on the back, since the backwall covers it all up.Â To solve that problem, take a look at this next shot, also from Ashfall.
Notice how the walkway branches off to the doorstep of this house?Â Would a nice walkway be built that leads to a wall?Â Probably not. They would probably lead to a door.Â This is another method, but you might need to do some explanation about this as well.Â If you think players will be too confused by these alternate facing doorways, take them out, and continue your life with the front-facing tradition.![](vault/images/32_9.jpg)
To figure out where doors are on the inside of houses, if you aren't using frontwalls and the door isn't visible on the backwall, put a doormat in the blackness surrounding the house right where the doormat is on the outside.Â That will tell without telling where the door is located.Â These tips and tricks will surely help you.
Town, Castle, and Cave Construction
Congragulations!Â You can now make realistic boards of houses, rooms, and other small places.Â But now, you must create a realistic collage of these boards, and transform them into whole towns, castles, caves, airports, whatever you want to call them.
You'll probably be making towns first.Â My observations of most towns, in many of the older Final Fantasy games and in practically all Toolkit games, is that everybody is homeless.Â In many towns, you have an inn, appropriate shops, maybe one or two houses of people who have an effect on the story, and a bunch of npcs walking around waiting for someone to come up to them so they can give this person advise.Â Do you see that happening in your neighborhood?Â C'mon, most of those wandering people need advise!
First off, you must figure what the town will have; weapon shops, inns, etc.Â You might also want to include other neccessities that towns must have as well, such as a warehouse, city hall, tavern, barns, guardhouses, the like.Â With all these industries, figure out how much people are needed to operate them.Â In Ashfall, my houses have anywhere from 2 to 6 people inhabiting them, 2-3 of them children (sometimes people live with their siblings if they are too poor to afford their own house).Â Thats roughly 2-4 adults per household. If your town's industries require about 30 people, dividing it by the average adult per household by 3, and you have 10 houses.Â Sometimes owners of shops live in their shops, which can cut down on the amount of houses littering your prized village.Â Now you have a realistic, balanced town to occupy your game.
Castles should be based off of the same concept.Â Figure how many soldiers, servents, etc., reside in your castle, and make the room to house them.Â Don't forget the royal family!Â In a lot of castles, however, the throne room is on the opposite side of the castle as the entrance, which is fine, yes, but when enemies storm the castle on the opposite side, who are they closest too?Â The king!Â I like having my throne room and the royal family's bedrooms in the center of the castle, but that is entirely preference.Â A lot of Toolkit games and many other commercial RPGs butcher the concept of castles.Â Castles are built as a symbol of the protection of the peasants. The castle is a fort built with many defenses, and has a floorplan designed with defense and protection in mind, not glamour and glory.Â If its a castle, it must retain that definition. However, if its a palace, then it would be glamourous and glorious. The concept should be true to the dictionary.
In many Toolkit games dealing with caves, these dark haunting caverns are maze-like, with dead-ends, traps, and ghosts.Â For one, not many of these are true.Â Yes, the townsfolk may say its dark and spooky, but the way the cave is actually designed and drawn it just looks like a big rocky mesa.Â Like I said in the overview , there must be a variety in the board to give it the ambience that it requires.Â Moss, really REALLY dark passages, murky water.Â Those make a spooky cave. Not some colorful rock. Even though I am going out of my bounds and going into actual tile design, still, good boards need tiles that fit.
The caves, taking the ambience out of mind and staring straight into the design, are always mazes and large rooms filled with nothing.Â The latter can easily be solved.Â Large caverns can be filled with skeletons, loose rock, moss, and many of the things I mentioned above. Mazes, on the other hand, arn't really maze like.Â Because its so bright you can easily find yourself around the cavern by looking ahead of yourself, a problem discovered especially at higher resolutions. There shouldn't be an overload of junctions, but the branches that are actually dead ends should go off for several boards, and, after fighting so many enemies and nearly exhausting your healing supplies, the player discovers it to be a dead-end.Â Thats a cave.Â That makes a good, fun, and hard level.
Overworld Design
The games that are actually long enough or are designed good enough to have overworlds, have a world that isn't very well designed or thought-out.Â Assume that most towns and villages take up a 50x50 board, and most towns on a world map uses one tile.Â That one overworld tile is the equivelent of a 50x50 tile board in real life porportions.Â Thus, using the 1 square meter to 1 tile comparison, 20 tiles on the overworld would make one kilometer. Thats slightly bigger then whats displayed at a 640x480 resolution. So, lets say the next town is on the next overworld board (using 50x50 boards), thats roughly 60-70 tiles of travel, maybe more. That amounts to 3.5 kilometers, a journey easily made in an hour, probably even with combat. Let me take a sample Toolkit game, "Never Ending Scroll".Â The first kingdom has one town under its control.Â The next town, which is presumably independent since no references are made that it belongs to any particular nation, is roughly 4 kilometers away using our distances and measurements described above.Â A kingdom, with a 2-3 kilometer radius (or for you Americans, 1.25-2 miles)?Â Sad sad indeed.
On the subject of kingdoms and their powers, to make a decent kingdom, one must have a castle, usually next to the big city of the land (the hub of trade for the region, so to speak). Towns, villages, and farms surround the castle/city for about a dozen miles, marking the power of the king.Â That is the average size of a kingdom in the dark ages, a relatively small kingdom in the middle ages and definetly in the renassance.Â In the sample game above, the second town could very well be part of the original kingdom, as a secondary trade hub, but it probably wasn't, for there wasn't any roads going back and forth between the first and second towns, and no one made any mention of that second town's existance.
I'll take a rather large city, roughly 100x100 in size, as my first city.Â On the overworld, that city still is represented by one tile.Â That means another city is, say, 80 tiles away, would be 8 kilometers away, which could be far enough away for it to count as an independent nation, especially if your world is relatively small (which I have no problem with). It could still account for the same kingdom, though, if you really wanted to.Â Even so, a kingdom can't really call itself one if there's only one city under its rule.
The actual design of the Earth is much more difficult.Â I have seen some unrealistic landscapes in some of the Toolkit games I have played, even in many of the Final Fantasy games.Â A good example to base your world off of is the world of Middle-Earth. There are actual mountain ranges, with river valleys running through them, and large basins with highlands and forests.Â Such a world would proove realistic, and fun, because long mountain ranges and rivers can proove quite a hassel.Â Also, I'd rather use a large board rather then multiple 50x50s.Â Yes, the loading time is undesirable, and naturally the enemy skill level won't go up for the internal battle system, but both of those are workable.
