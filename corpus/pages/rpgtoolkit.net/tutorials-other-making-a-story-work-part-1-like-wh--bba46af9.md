**Making A Story Work, Part 1**
Characterization: Personality & Interaction
John Doe Neat: 3/10 Outgoing: 8/10 Active: 5/10 Playful: 2/10 Nice: 4/10 Jane Smith Neat: 8/10 Outgoing: 2/10 Active: 10/10 Playful: 4/10 Nice: 4/10
Every now and then, someone in the TK community will release a game with a captivating storyline. However, these games are few and far between, much less often than it should. This series of articles will hopefully help you make your story one to remember.
What exactly makes a storyline great and what causes people to lose interest altogether? Even if you make the greatest storyline ever, it can still plummet to its foundation without some important factors to hold it together.
You all should be familiar will . The process of making characters. Unfortunately, it only begins there. Characterization involves everything from forming a character?s appearance, to knowing his or her stats (name, age, etc.), to creating the character?s personal background. Characterization is a large subject, so I'll break it into important points. ***characterization***
Characterization is global, meaning that it doesn't end with a drawing or a table of statistics. You can create truly unique characters, but you need to bring them alive in the game. This brings us to . ***personality***
If a character in a game doesn't have a personality, they are very boring when you talk to them. Even NPCs should have a general personality. There are several ways to help you make a character's personality. A good example is in , where personality is ranked in a table in such categories as neat, active, and outgoing. A simple chart like this is a good place to start for your characters. This chart can tell you what to make their houses like, what their jobs and hobbies are, how they act around certain people, and how they react to the current surroundings. Observe: *The Sims*
From this chart, we know a lot about John without ever meeting him. We know that his house would not be very clean, and he would favor unclean surroundings over clean areas. We know that he is serious most of the time, and isn?t very nice, unless he?s flirting with a girl. He isn?t more or less active than the average person. He?ll do some stuff, but takes breaks and gets lazy.
One thing I notice most about TK games lately is that the characters take their surroundings for granted. Use a chart like this to liven the feel of your game. If John meets a girl that joins his party, you can except that he?ll hit on the girl often. If a guy then enters the party, expect John to pay less attention to him than the girl. If this party enters a small unkempt village, we know that John would feel right at home, but if they enter a large clean city, he?ll feel uneasy.
Another way to characterize your characters is . So what if John reacts to certain things, if there is no interaction? You should make your characters react with others and causes friendships or rivalries. For example, let?s add another character: ***interaction***
If Jane and John meet, their personalities will cause arguments. Jane likes things to be clean, and John prefers dirty areas. Jane isn?t very outgoing, so she won?t appreciate John flirting with her. Jane will be active almost all of the time, and John may mention that she works too hard, and needs to relax. Since Jane isn?t very playful, she?ll probably take this as an insult, especially since she isn?t very nice.
This isn't the only way to create a character's personality. I personally use an Excel chart with loads of fields varying from 'Height' to 'Eye Color' to 'Weapons'. All of these things can help create a character's personality, and they also give you lots of opportunities for observation between your interacting characters.
**Making A Story Work, Part 2**
** Characterization: Decisions and Conflict
In most TK games I've played, characters do stuff without a thought or a worry. This is bad. Even game characters deserve to think for themselves, even if we program their thoughts. Continuing on the subject of characterization, today we will make our characters more human.
You decide what to eat for breakfast. You decide what to wear each day. You decide whether to drink so many shots of...well, you get the idea. You make decisions everyday. Lots of them. Why should your characters be denied this privelige? Although I may sound like a character rights activist right now, can make your games much more fun to play. ***decision making***
We all know what a decision is. You face a multitude of choices, and you have to decide which to do. Think of what kind of decisions your character in your game could face. Perhaps there are two cities that your party can travel to. Which should the party visit first? Maybe two party members are at odds, and forcing you to side with one or the other. Who does your character pick?
The best TK game I have played so far had a much more enjoyable story because their characters made decisions on the fly. is a perfect example of what an impact decision making can have on your story. The characters become more personable and likeable. In this game, the characters not only make decisions by themselves, but weigh the consequences of all possible outcomes and choose the best route. More TK games need to incorporate this tactic. *Andromeda Sky: Angel Tears*
There's a bully at school who wants your spleen visible. Your algebra teacher hates you and adjusts your grade accordingly. In addition to making decisions everyday, you face a lot of . ***conflicts***
There are two kinds of conflcits. External conflicts, the most common, involve a person or a gorup of people v. another person, a person or group of people v. nature, or a person or group of people v. another group of people. These are your basic good versus evil conflicts in most cliched games. Your party generally is viewed as 'good' and your opposition is 'evil'.
Internal conflicts involve a person v. him/herself or a group of people v. themselves. The easiest form of internal conflict would be a civil war. However, the best internal conflicts reside within a person. Guilt, worry, and grief can all be internal conflicts.
The best example of conflicts I have played is . You have external conlficts such as Party v. Sin, Tidus v. Jecht, Yuna v. Dona, Party v. Other summoner parties, and Party v. Seymour. You also have internal conflicts such as Tidus v. himself (getting back home), Tidus v. Yuna (internal because Tidus worries if he should tell Yuna the truth about Jecht.), Auron v. living, Wakka v. Tidus & Chappu, and Rikku v. fear of lightning (yes, fear is an internal conflict). *Final Fantasy X*
As you can see, storylines rely on both internal and external conflicts. Without these conflicts, FFX would've been another boring nameless RPG (although still a FF, so not nameless). Internal conflicts aren't as hard to add into your game as you may think. A good rule is to give every character at least one internal conflict, whether it is a fear, a worry, a doubt, or a disease. Mental diseases are great for conflicts, since they have the person fighting against themselves and against those around them.
Well, there you have it. Two more great ways to improve your storyline. Decision making is great way to liven up an otherwise dull cutscene. for any game and you can carry internal conflicts subtlely throughout the game without revealing them. Together, decision making and conflicts make your charcters more human. **Conflicts are necessary**
