Here is first: LETTERS Lincesqua'n alphabet basicaly resembles typical english alphabet based on latin characters, but there are less letters. Because you propably know all letters there are in english, I'll just list letters which are NOT in Lincesqua. There are 2 such letters. K - is unneeded, as "C" takes it's place. Y - is usually unused and it it's place, there is usually "I". How to read all the other? Use spanish/italian/latin style of reading, with the exception of few cases I guess I should explain... C - is ALWAYS readed as K, like in ancient greek. "Ce" (what) is readed as "Ke", not as "Se" or "Ce"? I - there are 2 types of I, soft I and hard I. Hard I exists as a separate vovel, while soft I is sort of "finishing" or "softening". Example of hard I can be first I in "Iulia" (second one is soft), soft I's exist in word combinations like "li", "ni", "ti" etc and always in the end of suffix of every verb (videmi, vadeti) or genitive male form of adjective ending with -i. S - is ALWAYS readed as soft S, never Z. QU - is readed as "Ku" not "Kv" like in latin. "Aqua" is readed as "Akua" not as "Akva". Z - is taking S' place in some hard-S words, like Roza, not Rosa (a rose). It's also common at beginning of words like Zebra and such. The only word combinations beside "QU" in Lincesqua are CH and SH which represent the same as in english. Altough not existing in natural words, they can be used from custom, other-language words like Chechenia. To use custom words from other languages like names of people, places, etc eliminate all the additional combinations so that words is written same as it is readed in Lincesqua (Moebius = Mobius, Cathedra = Catedra). NOUN DECLENSION I Each word in Lincesqua is built from 2 parts: the topic, and the suffix. Let's take the word "street" - "strada". The "strad" part is a topic which never changes during declension.* What changes is the suffix - "a". For each case, the suffix takes different form. * For some words, topic changes a bit in singular nominative form compared to other cases. There are 4 cases for all nouns in Lincesqua. NOMINATIVE is basicaly the basic word's name, like street. It's also used to answer simple questions like "what?", like I see (what?) street. GENITIVE is used for possesion, like something of street, belonging to street. DATIVE shows target of action, like when someone does something to street. INSTRUMENTAL can be also known as ABLATIVE but INSTRUMENTAL sounds funnier. It shows an object action is performed with, like doing something using the street, with street. It is also used always with word "with", like town with long streets or dog with long tail. It is also used as locative form (I was going through street can be translated as "I was going (with) street" with instrumental form). Finally, it is used after words indicating being or becoming something ("he became a teacher", "it is a dog") when not used in global form (like "it's a dog, that's a dog") but rather in personal form ("this thing is a dog"). Personal form is also used (along with instrumental form) when refering to some subject a second time ("this is Max, Max is a dog"). There are of course questions for each case... Nominative Se? Ce? (Who? What?) Genitive Sei? Cei? (Of who? Of what?) Dative Seu? Ceu? (To who? To what?) Instrumental Seum? Ceum? (With who? With what?) Beside using instrumental case after "with" word (in Lincesqua, "con") and after being/becoming type words when refering to personal subject, there aren't any other especially hard irregularities. Words "of", "to" and "with" are not required before various case words, except - as usually - the word "with" when - and only when - a word also means reverse possesion and something belonging to something, like dog with a tail or man with glasses. To put this up easily... I see with eyes I see (instrumental) eyes I go through street I go (instrumental) street Dog with a bone Dog WITH (instrumental) bone There are 2 numbers in Lincesqua: singular and plural, and 3 genders: male, female, neutral. Declination I is the basic (mostly female noun) declination. There are 2 types of this declination. Now, onto declination tables themselves! TYPE I Type I of noun declension I is used for female words ending with -a, like the exemplar "strada". The table itself looks like this: Sin Pl Nom -a -e Gen -e -i Dat -i -am Ins -u -ami Adding topics to the changing suffixes we get... Sin Pl Nom Strada Strade - Street, Streets Gen Strade Stradi - Of street, of streets Dat Stradi Stradam - To street, to streets Ins Stradu Stradami - With street, with streets To bring you some words to work on... NOUNS*: Strada, strade - Street, road (f) Puella, puelle - Girl (f) Roza, roze - Rose (f) Eda, ede - She (f) Unlike most languages, nouns like he/she/it are declensioned regularily in Lincesqua. There are also NO prefixes in Lincesqua (like the / a). A word is a word, it's not "the word" or "a word", it's just "word". * Each noun presented will be done so with nomative and genitive (or nominative plural, these forms are ALWAYS the same for same word in Lincesqua) so that's it's easily possible to notice what is a suffix and what is a topicin the certain word. VERBS: Vadeti - (he/she/it) walks (from "vadete", "to walk") Vademi - (I) walk Videti - (he/she/it) sees (from "videte", "to see") Videmi - (I) see And thus, few sentences to explain this all far more easily: (I) see street. - Videmi strata. Rose (of) girl / Girl's rose. - Roza puelle. (He) walks (through the) street. - Vadeti stradu. (I) walk (through the) streets. - Vademi stradami. Girl with roses sees street. - Puella con rozami videti strada. Simple, isn't it? You just need to know which case to use for what question. TYPE II Type II of declension looks very similar to type I, except it's for both male and female words ending with -er and the suffix looks different for singular nominative case compared to others. A typical word of this declension can be "mater" - "mother". The noun "mater" has a topic of "matr" in all cases beside singular nominative. So it's "mater, matre" and NOT "mater, matere". The table looks like... Sin Pl Nom -(er) -(r)e Gen -(r)e -(r)i Dat -(r)i -(r)am Ins -(r)u -(r)ami To understand it better, you can assume mat- is topic and -er is simply nominative singular suffix, then add r before each other suffix of table same in type I. The declension for that words looks like this anyway... Sin Pl Nom Mater Matre Gen Matre Matri Dat Matri Matram Ins Matru Matrami Here are some words and sentences to present: NOUNS: Mater, matre - Mother (f) Pater, patre - Father (m) Brater, bratre - Brother (m) Svaster, svastre - Sister (f) As you can see, these are all family member names. And these are mainly the only words for this declension. Other -er ending words are declined in other main declensions for respective genders (like name "Peter" or job "magister" - teacher) just with the same topic irregularily ("petr-", "magistr-"). VERBS: Eti - (he/she/it )is (from "ete", "be") Mother (of) girl walks (through the) street. - Mater puelle vadeti stradu. She is (a) sister (of) father. - Eda eti svastru patre. And this is, I guess, all, for now.
