I recommend trying to stay away from the little green men theory. There's no doubt that there are aliens in the literal sense, in that there is life other than on planet earth, but I find it highly unlikely that there is intelligent life, and I think your essay would be more interesting if you went with the part of the subject that most people haven't heard a thousand times before.
Suppose there is some incredibly large number of planets X. We are thinking about a very, very, very large number here. There are a lot of planets. Then suppose there is some number Y which is the chance of a self-replicating entity arising spontaneously. This is pretty unlikely but it is very likely compared to some other things (e.g. the earth decaying right now). Then calculate the expected number of planets with life. We would expect to see it on quite a few planets indeed. Many scientists actually find the idea of intelligent life on other planets to be quite likely, hence the idea of SETI, etc.

I'm not sure what most scientists consider "intelligent life", but lets say for example humans are the only form of intelligent life on Earth, now consider how many other forms of life there are on earth, and the chances that there is another planet in our solar system that just happens to support life. It seems pretty unlikely to me. I'm not saying it's impossible, just extremely unlikely. How do we know that? We only just recently found one or two others in our own solar system, so how do we know for sure there are lots more?

Cosmology is a vast subject. There are many ways we can asses the number of planets; the formation of galaxies tends to form a ballpark number of planets which we can estimate from gravitational effects in the our own galaxy. We can also estimate the number of galaxies based on how the initialal gravitation singularity expanded, but even just counting galaxies we can 'see', there are quite a few, each with unspeakable numbers of planets. Of course, I use 'planet' is a liberal sense; I am referring to large chucks of matter in orbit around stars, not the literal meaning of 'planet', which is specific to our star system (the solar system). As for intelligent life, it is not very unlikely, given enough time. There is no chance involved in natural selection. There is chance in the random mutations that natural selection has to work with, but because we have lots of time to work with, the evolution of 'intelligence' can indeed be long and drawn out so that any one step along the way is very likely; natural selection ensures that each step is followed, rather than a random direction, because even some very superficial intelligence is better than no intelligence. So, because the universe has been around for 13 billion years (this is a result from cosmology too) and that there are vast numbers of planet-like masses around, and because of natural selection, the existence of intelligent life is not too implausible. One more thing to keep in mind is that the speed of light, c, is not just a 'speed limit' for masses; it is the limit that can travel if we want to maintain our familiar laws of causality, which do seem to exist. In other words, if we suspected a far away planet contained life and we were able to zoom in on it with an incredible telescope, we would see the planet as it existed a long time ago because the information of the new 'state' of the planet could not have been transmitted to us yet. To summarise, the chance of intelligent life existing elsewhere in the universe is very high but the chance of us finding any indication of that life is very low. However, SETI considers it relatively likely that we will pick up electromagnetic signals, which travel at the speed c, from long dead alien civilisations, which would be cool.
