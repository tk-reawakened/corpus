# the beginning...

About Us
**the beginning...**
What is the RPG Toolkit all about? That is best answered by who it's about... My name is Chris Matthews. I live in Halifax, Nova Scotia, Canada. I'm just recently graduated from . But my story begins before I started university. My story starts the same as many people like me. When I was 10, I saved up my money and bought one of the world's greatest computers-- a Commodore 64. It was fantastic for playing games (and I still play a few with an emulator), but what really fascinated me was that I could make the computer do what I wanted it to do. The C64 manuals were excellent and provided a really good introduction to programming. I created a number of games with my C64. Most of them were text adventures. In 1992 I made my first real game: a text adventure called The Jewel Of Destiny. I created it under the production name of Awesome Software. The game even had the sword logo like you see above (except that it was a text-based graphic :) Awesome Software was born...![The old Awesome Software logo](http://toolkitzone.com/vault/documents/1089.jpg) [Dalhousie University](http://www.dal.ca/)
**early video games**
I've always wanted to make video games. It's not easy. I learned that very quickly when I started programming. That's why I only ever made text adventures. How could I ever make a graphics-based game? Eventually I moved onto the PC platform and begam playing around with gwbasic (it came with ms-dos 3). But after awhile we got ms-dos 5, which came packaged with QBasic. Good old QBasic! QBasic made graphics programming a lot easier than the archaic style of programming that the C64 used. I played around with graphics programming, but it was never really easy. I still couldn't see myself making a real game. In 1995 I bought a registered version of the highly popular game engine. ZZT was an ASCII-Based game that included an editor that allowed you to make your own games. My friend and I tried to make our own ZZT game called the . As was my style, it was created under the Awesome Software logo. It was a pretty good game (as far as ZZT went), but we never finished it because of the limitations ZZT placed upon the game. We wanted to make our game much larger than was possible, so it remains unfinished to this day.

**the toolkit is born**
After The Gate of Time, I was looking in an old Nintendo Power one day and saw an article on games that were released in Japan but which never made it to America. One of them was a game engine which allowed you to create a space shoot-em-up style game. There was a screenshot of the tile editor with a ship being drawn in the grid. I thought that was pretty cool. So I fired up QBasic and tried my hand at making a tile editor like the one I saw in the magazine. That was in 1996. When the tile editor was done I made a companion program that would let you place those tiles on the screen and make scenes. Unwittingly I had just designed the first two parts of the RPG Toolkit Development System (a 'temporary' name I gave to the project). And, of course, the Toolkit was being produced as an Awesome Software product. As I put more work into the Toolkit, it started to take on a life of its own. Many features 'designed themselves'. I always thought about things like 'what if I wanted to do this in a game', and then I would put an option into the Toolkit to let me do it. As a consequence, the Toolkit eventually became a very flexible RPG engine. It took me 9 months to program the first version of the Toolkit-- editor, engine and all (with fighting and the RPGCode scripting language). That was my first real project. I was 16. After 6 years of playing around with programming, I was finally able to make my own game from start to finish. We were supposed to do a re-make of the Gate of Time with the Toolkit, but it never happened.![](http://toolkitzone.com/vault/documents/1091.jpg)

**the internet**
In January 1997, the Toolkit was done. But so what? Who knew about it? I was a kid working on a hobby. Who would care about that? Well, in March of that year, my family got the Internet. We had a 386 with a 2600bps modem (yes, that's 0.26k/s). I tried my hand at some HTML and posted a website on the internet-- Awesome Software Online. I posted a few screenshots of the Toolkit, but that was about it. I think I got 100 hits in the first few months. They were all me :) The internet opened up a world of possibilities to me. For the first time, I saw that there were other people out there like me who played around with QBasic and tried to make games. That was cool. I found compilers for Basic on the internet and even tried compiling the Toolkit into an EXE. That was kinda cool! In the meantime, Awesome Software Online changed very little. But I started to get hits from other people. Near the end of 1997, I was actually getting emails asking if the Toolkit could be released. Amazed that someone else took an interest in my hobby, I posted the RPG Toolkit Development System, Version 1.2d on the Internet. Stuff happened. People downloaded it. People gave me suggestions and found bugs. I fixed the bugs and implemented the suggestions as best I could. That was a fun time. Awesome Software had grown into a nice little community of people who emailed me regularly, talked to me on ICQ, etc. I was very happy. Just to think- the small experiment I had tried on a small 8086 computer two years ago had grown into something pretty big. I once had a guy tell me on ICQ that I was lucky- lucky for all the people who emailed me and asked me questions- complete strangers from all over the world who I came to know pretty good. And even now, I can see that I really am lucky. Newer versions of the Toolkit came out. Version 1.4 was the last release of the Old Toolkit. In the Summer of 1998 it became obvious that the Toolkit had to be completely redesigned. DOS was dying. And version 1's source code was so messy I didn't even want to look at it (I had variables in there called 'bob'. What does 'bob' do? I don't know!)
**version 2**
I started work on a new version of the Toolkit. It was windows-based. Everything was from scratch. I simply maintained the principles I had used in version 1 as a guide. I worked on it during my first year of university, and it was really fun (much better than the stuff they were getting me to do in class, like tic tac toe). In February of 1999, Beta 1 of the RPG Toolkit was released... The Toolkit really took off after that. There were very few RPG engines on the Internet at that time, and certainly very few that were Windows based. The Toolkit filled a need, and a lot of people came on board. My site saw phenomenal growth at the release, and the hits to the sit just continued to grow. Version 2 became the standard for most people. It taught a lot of people how to program, which I think is pretty cool. It's nice to know that I've introduced a lot of people to something they might not have considered exploring before. Version 2 went through of revisions. There were 20 major releases (the final release was 2.20b in the summer of 2002). Some of those releases had big enough improvements that the system rightly should have been re-labelled version 3 or version 4. Version 2 is available in 4 languages, so it really does appeal to a worldwide audience. Many many many games were created with version 2. Many more than I ever expected. At the time of this writing, there have been nearly half a million downloads. My little hobby really took off, and I'm really greatful to everyone for their support!

**awesome computing**
In August, 2000, I registered a business in my home province of Nova Scotia. I named it Awesome Computing, since Awesome Software turned out to be the name of a company in Ontario. The reason I registered my business is because the Toolkit had grown so popular, it was actually starting to cost a lot of money to keep going. When my website first started, it was hosted on my family's webspace with our ISP. Later I moved it to the now defunct xoom.com. Finally I purchansed the rpgtoolkit.com domain and started paying for hosting. Further, to meet the feature requirements of the users, I had to buy several development tools like a modern version of Visual Basic and Visual C++. I had to buy licenses to programming libraries that I was using to bring everyone more features. It was really quite expensive, and it continued to be. I love the users. They have always been incredibly loyal, and have always helped me see the value in offering the Toolkit to anyone, anywhere. And when I started to offer a registered version for sale, I received great support from the community. I continued to offer a Standard Edition for anyone to use 100% freely. I believe that everyone should be able to make their own games. But the Powerhouse Edition. added a few more features and a license to re-sell games made with the Toolkit, for anyone who is an aspiring entrepreneur (which I have to tell you is very rewarding work). I don't make a lot of money. I'm a single guy developing a program that thousands of people enjoy. And a lot of users support me, and for that I am extremely greatful. From my perspective, it makes a lot more sense to support a software developer who is directly in touch with his users than some facless corporation. Fortunately, others share that view, too (by the way, if you help me out, I would greatly appreciate it. You can buy the Powerhouse Edition or even leave a small donation-- it would really help me out!)

**toolkit 3**
In the summer or 2002, the Toolkit (version 2) had undergone 20 major revisions. My education from university made me aware of a lot of the flaws inherent in the design and how it would be difficult to continue development on the same codebase into the future. By this time, lots of RPG engines had popped up on the internet. Some were powerful. Some were vapour. In any event, the Toolkit needed a major overhaul to bring it a newer and more modern set of features. So I began coding version 3 (now referred to as TK3). TK3 is the result of nearly a year of solid development. Much of the version 2 code has been removed completely and rewritten with a fresh new outlook. It uses features available to modern systems like hardware rendering and advanced sound. TK3 is truly a powerful system and I am extremely proud to be offering it to you all to use. At the time of this writing, TK3 is still in it's infancy, so I can't wait to see what the future holds. But I'm sure that the community will continue to support my efforts. Whatever happens, I am content in knowing that at last I managed to create my own games; and in so doing, I've helped thousands of others do the same. I can't take the credit for that. If it wasn't for the interest of everyone out there, version 1.2b would still be sitting on my harddrive, uncompiled, and unused. So what is this all about? Like I said, it's not what it is, but who it is. We are all part of that who in one way or another, and I for one, thank you all for being part of this.

**open source**
The Toolkit went open source in late 2003 and after a slow start development picked up pace after Toolkit Zone became the official site. Over the months a number of contributors, most notably Delano and Colin, have pushed the software further and further. Stability, object-orientated programming, full isometrics...it's all there.
